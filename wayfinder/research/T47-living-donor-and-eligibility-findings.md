# T47 Living-Donor Safety & Donor Eligibility — Research Findings

Closes T01's Gap **G7** (myths with no ODF/SATS or Source Corpus grounding) for Beginner Level Stage 2, myth #11 ("past illness or a cancer history excludes you") and myth #12 ("living donation is unsafe for the donor"). It also closes Gap **G3** (the ODF Tissue Bank FAQ was never fetched) so that myth #10 can cite directly. Per CONTEXT.md, this is source material for lesson drafters, not lesson copy.

## Method notes

- **Labels.** Every claim carries one of two tags:
  - **[SA]**: a South African primary source (ODF, SATS/SATCS, the National Health Act and its regulations, an SA hospital or provincial document, or peer-reviewed SA literature).
  - **📌 [non-SA]**: the spec's Source Note. The claim comes from a non-South-African source, so lesson drafters must not present it as South African fact.
- **ODF pages were fetched as raw HTML and the text extracted locally.** A summarising fetch tool truncated and paraphrased the ODF answers, so the quotes below come from the page source itself. They are verbatim as of 2026-09-23.
- **SATS position statements** sit behind a download gate on sats.org.za/position-statements/ and sats.org.za/resources/. They were downloaded and read in full. The Groote Schuur KPD SOP (19 pp.) turned out to contain a donor-facing living kidney donor risk brochure. It is the most useful single SA document in this ticket.
- **The SATCS *Organ and Tissue Donation Reference File* ("Red File", Source Corpus Document H)** was re-read for eligibility content. Page numbers below are the file's own printed page numbers, which match T06's "Red File p.41" usage. The file is undated; its newest reference is 2021.
- **The National Health Act 61 of 2003** (gov.za, a61-03.pdf) and **GN R180 of 2 March 2012** (as hosted by SATS; Sabinet reproduction of Gazette 35099) were read directly. Statutory quotes are verbatim.
- **The 2026 draft organ-transplant regulations** (GN 7879, Gazette 55299, 4 September 2026) are a scanned PDF with no text layer. A sub-agent read all 24 pages visually. The cover page and regs 10–11 were then re-checked against rendered page images for this file, so those quotes are verified. The other draft quotes (Annexures A and B, regs 13 and 27) are the sub-agent's reading.
- **International figures** were checked against the MEDLINE abstract (NCBI E-utilities) or the full text where reachable. Paywalled full texts are named as such. The SaBTO and NHSBT documents were read in full as PDFs. The EDQM/Council of Europe organ guide sits behind a bot challenge and could not be read (see Gaps).
- Sub-agents did parts of this research: living-kidney figures, living-liver figures, the 2012 notices and the 2026 draft, and the cancer, HIV and hepatitis guidance. The living-liver figures come from a sub-agent pass checked against the abstracts, and are recorded here as that pass reported them. Figures from the SATS 5-year report were re-checked here against the PDF's text layer.

## 1. Gap G3 closed: what the ODF Tissue Bank FAQ actually says

**odf.org.za/tissue-bank-faqs/ was fetched in full. It does not contain any of the three myth-#10 points** ("no perfect body needed", "possible after death has already occurred", "doesn't preclude funeral rites"). T01 §1 item 10 assumed it did, without fetching it. The page has four questions: what a tissue bank is, which banks operate in SA, the tissue-bank process, and which tissue can be donated.

What the page *does* say that is usable **[SA]**, from the ODF Tissue Bank FAQ:
- On disfigurement: "Retrieval officers make use of prosthesis where necessary to ensure no disfigurement of the donor body."
- On skin: "When skin is donated, only a very thin layer (similar to tissue paper) is removed and the area from which it is retrieved looks like a light graze."
- On screening: "Donor suitability is initially assessed by means of a comprehensive screening process that includes the evaluation of the medical and social history and testing of blood and tissue samples." This is useful for myth #11.
- SA tissue banks named: Gauteng Cornea & Eye Bank (Johannesburg), Eye Bank Foundation of SA (Cape Town), KZN Cornea & Eye Association (Durban), UFS Tissue Lab (Bloemfontein, cardiovascular), Centre for Tissue Engineering (Pretoria, multi-tissue) and Vitanova (Cape Town, multi-tissue).

**Where myth #10's three points are actually grounded** (all [SA]):

| Myth #10 point | Citable SA source (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| Tissue donation happens *after* death, unlike organ donation | ODF FAQ, "What is the difference between organ and tissue donation": "An organ transplant takes place after an individual has been declared brain dead but is still being supported on a respirator, whereas tissue retrieval can still take place several hours and even days after death." ODF FAQ, "How soon after death…": "Many people die at work or on the roads and never make it to the hospital. In such instances an organ donation would not be possible, however, a tissue donation … can be successfully carried out." Red File p.15: "Tissue donation can take place irrespective of the manner of death." |
| Doesn't preclude funeral rites or viewing | ODF FAQ, "Does being a donor delay the funeral?": "No. As soon as the donated organs / tissue has been removed, the body is returned to the family to bury or cremate." ODF Cornea FAQ: "Any funeral option is available to the family, including a viewing"; "Because the procurement is performed mere hours after death, there is no delay in funeral procedures." Red File p.16: "the body is restored to its original state with the aid of prostheses post-recovery. An open casket funeral is possible after tissue recovery." |
| No "perfect body" needed | **No source uses this phrase.** The closest grounding: Red File p.15, "While exclusion criteria and safety protocols apply, many more people can donate tissue, than people able to donate organs"; Red File p.21, "Most patients who pass away can be tissue donors"; ODF Cornea FAQ, "corneas can be used even if a donor wears glasses", and "Cataracts and poor eyesight do not prevent cornea donation" (Red File p.21). Draft the point as "far more people can donate tissue than organs; poor eyesight doesn't stop cornea donation". Don't use the words "perfect body" as though a source said them. |

## 2. Myth #12: "Living donation is unsafe for the donor"

### 2.1 What ODF and SATS say

- **ODF [SA]**: one sentence, and it says nothing about donor risk. ODF FAQ, "Can I donate an organ / tissue while I am alive?": "Yes, in some cases. Live donations, such as a kidney are often done between family members, because the blood groups and tissue types are more compatible. This ensures a higher success rate." **ODF publishes nothing on living-donor safety.**
- **SATS [SA]**: SATS hosts a **donor-facing risk statement** as part of the Groote Schuur Hospital (GSH) *Standard Operating Procedure: Kidney Paired Donation Transplant Exchange Program* (dated 23 May 2025, review date 1 May 2030, compiled by Barday, Wearne, Thomson, Du Toit and McCurdie; Western Cape DoH letterhead), downloadable from sats.org.za/position-statements/. Its **Annexure G, "Groote Schuur Hospital Living Kidney Donor Operation – Information Brochure"**, p.2, reads verbatim:
  > "The operation is regarded as a safe and low-risk operation, but every surgery has some risks. Very rarely, bleeding may develop in or after your operation, requiring an urgent reoperation or blood transfusion. At times, we may not be able to complete the operation with the camera through keyhole surgery and may have to make an extra cut to safely proceed with your operation. Hernias and wound infections may develop but are rare."
  > "You will have a minimal risk of developing long-term kidney issues, but regular follow-ups will be performed…"
  > "Is there a risk of death? There is always a risk of death with any major operation. However, the risk of death from surgery for living kidney donors is very low. Living donors undergo careful pre-operative testing and evaluation to make sure they are healthy enough for surgery. In one study of over 80,000 living kidney donors, death from surgery was 3.1 per 10,000 donors, so less than 1 in 1000 donors. This rate has not changed for the last 15 years and is less than your chance of dying in a motor vehicle accident."

  Annexure G p.1 gives the operation plainly: general anaesthetic "for about 2 to 4 hours"; keyhole surgery through "three keyhole incisions of about 1cm each" plus a lower-abdominal incision; "about 3-5 days" in hospital; a check-up on day 8; "Most patients are ready to return to work after 6 weeks". It also says: "we always leave the stronger kidney with the donor", and "The remaining kidney typically provides enough function to maintain the donor's long-term health."

  **Drafting caution.** The brochure's "3.1 per 10,000 … has not changed for the last 15 years" is the Segev 2010 figure (§2.2). It describes 1994–2009 US data and is now superseded by a lower figure. The brochure's road-accident comparison is one the UK national guideline says "should be used with caution" (§2.2). Cite the brochure for what SA clinicians tell SA donors. Cite the registry directly for the number.

### 2.2 Living kidney donor: perioperative (surgical) mortality

- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]** Segev DL et al., "Perioperative mortality and long-term survival following live kidney donation," *JAMA* 2010;303(10):959–966, doi:10.1001/jama.2010.237. US OPTN registry, **80,347 live kidney donors, 1 Apr 1994–31 Mar 2009**: "There were 25 deaths within 90 days of live kidney donation during the study period. Surgical mortality from live kidney donation was **3.1 per 10,000 donors** (95% confidence interval [CI], 2.0-4.6) and did not change during the last 15 years despite differences in practice and selection." The paper also found subgroup variation: men 5.1 vs women 1.7 per 10,000, and donors with hypertension 36.7 vs 1.3 per 10,000. **The "roughly 3 per 10,000" figure is exactly this: 3.1 per 10,000.**
- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]: the current figure is lower.** Massie AB, Motter JD, Snyder JJ, Levan ML, Segev DL, "Thirty-Year Trends in Perioperative Mortality Risk for Living Kidney Donors," *JAMA* 2024;332(12):1015–1017, doi:10.1001/jama.2024.14527. SRTR, **164,593 US living kidney donors, 1993–2022**: 36 deaths within 90 days (2.2 per 10,000 overall). By decade, 1993–2002 was 3.0 and 2003–2012 was 2.9, "and then declined statistically significantly in 2013-2022 (5 deaths, **0.9** [95% CI, 0.3-2.0] **per 10 000**; P = .01)". The authors conclude: "Current guidelines for donor informed consent, based on 2009 data, should be updated to reflect this information." The most common known cause of death was haemorrhage.
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK]** British Transplantation Society / Renal Association, *UK Guidelines for Living Donor Kidney Transplantation*, 4th ed., March 2018, §6.3 (summary: *Transplantation* 2018;102(7):e307, doi:10.1097/TP.0000000000002253). The guideline quotes "approximately 1 in 3,000", but explicitly as a figure from older **US** studies of *open* nephrectomy, so it is not a UK figure. The UK's own registry: "a study published in 2007 of 2,509 donors showed no peri-operative deaths". On road-accident analogies: "Such analogies should be used with caution."

**For the Beginner Level:** "about 3 in 10,000" is the widely quoted historical figure. The best current registry estimate is under 1 in 10,000. Both are 📌 US figures, and **no South African rate exists** (§2.5).

### 2.3 Living kidney donor: complications

- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]** Lentine KL et al., "Perioperative Complications After Living Kidney Donation: A National Study," *Am J Transplant* 2016;16(6):1848–1857, doi:10.1111/ajt.13687. 14,964 donors, 2008–2012: "Overall, **16.8%** of donors experienced a perioperative complication… Major Clavien Classification of Surgical Complications grade IV or higher affected **2.5%** of donors." (Abstract only; the full text was paywalled. A "7.3% grade III" figure quoted secondhand in the UK guideline could not be checked against the paper's tables, so don't use it.)
- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]** Lentine KL, Lam NN, Segev DL, "Risks of Living Kidney Donation," *Clin J Am Soc Nephrol* 2019;14(4):597–608, doi:10.2215/CJN.11220918: "Minor perioperative complications occur in 10%-20% of donor nephrectomy cases, but major complications occur in <3%, and the risk of perioperative death is <0.03%." **This is the cleanest one-line summary for lesson prose.**
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK]** BTS/RA 2018 §6.4, UK registry: major morbidity 4.5% after laparoscopic and 5.1% after open donor nephrectomy.

### 2.4 Living kidney donor: long-term outcomes

- 📌 **[non-SA, USA] Kidney failure (ESRD).** Muzaale AD et al., "Risk of end-stage renal disease following live kidney donation," *JAMA* 2014;311(6):579–586, doi:10.1001/jama.2013.285141. 96,217 donors, 1994–2011, compared with matched healthy non-donors from NHANES III: "Estimated risk of ESRD at 15 years after donation was **30.8 per 10,000** … in kidney donors and **3.9 per 10,000** … in their matched healthy nondonor counterparts"; "Estimated lifetime risk of ESRD was **90 per 10,000 donors, 326 per 10,000 unscreened nondonors (general population), and 14 per 10,000 healthy nondonors**"; "the magnitude of the absolute risk increase was small." In other words, donors' lifetime risk is higher than that of equally healthy non-donors, but well below the general population's, because donors are screened.
- 📌 **[non-SA, Norway]** Mjøen G et al., "Long-term risks for kidney donors," *Kidney Int* 2014;86(1):162–167, doi:10.1038/ki.2013.460. 1,901 donors, 1963–2007, median follow-up 15.1 years, against 32,621 donation-eligible controls: ESRD HR 11.38 (4.37–29.6), all-cause death HR **1.30** (1.11–1.52), cardiovascular death HR 1.40 (1.03–1.91). (Abstract only.) The ESRD HR rests on 9 cases. "302 cases per million" is a per-year rate, not a cumulative risk.
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK] Neutral summary of the two.** BTS/RA 2018: "Both are reassuring, indicating that the lifetime risk of ESRD after kidney donation is low, occurring in **less than 1:200 donors (0.5%)**. However, it appears clear that, for an individual at low baseline risk, donating a kidney does increase the risk of later developing ESRD… By contrast, it is not clear that kidney donation has a detrimental effect on long-term cardiovascular or all-cause mortality, with conflicting results from the US and Norwegian studies." The guideline also notes that most ESRD in donors occurred among **biological relatives** of the recipient (80% of Norwegian donors were first-degree relatives, and all the ESRD cases were in that group).
- **Life expectancy: the evidence is unresolved, and the course should say so.** Segev 2010 (📌 USA) found long-term mortality "no higher" than matched controls at a median of 6.3 years. Mjøen 2014 (📌 Norway) found a 30% relative excess at a median of 15 years, against an imperfect control group. **Don't teach "donation doesn't shorten your life" or "it does".**
- 📌 **[non-SA, Canada] Pregnancy.** Garg AX et al., *N Engl J Med* 2015;372(2):124–133, doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1408932: gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia in 11% of donor pregnancies vs 5% in non-donors (OR 2.4). "Most women had uncomplicated pregnancies after donation."
- 📌 **[non-SA, 28-country meta-analysis] Blood pressure.** Boudville N et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2006;145(3):185–196: "a 5-mm Hg increase in blood pressure within 5 to 10 years after donation over that anticipated with normal aging."

### 2.5 South African living-kidney-donor outcome data

- **[SA]** Dayal C, Davies M, Diana NE, Meyers A, "Living kidney donation in a developing country," *PLoS ONE* 2022;17(5):e0268183, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0268183. **Charlotte Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital**, all potential living donors assessed 1 Jan 1981–31 Jul 2015. **This is the only peer-reviewed SA living-kidney-donor outcome series found.** Verified against the full text:
  - 1,208 potential donors were evaluated; **298 (24.7%) went on to donate** and 910 did not. "Medical contraindications to donation were the most common cause for donor disqualification (n = 365; 40.1%)", most often "obesity, hypertension, renal dysfunction, and HIV infection". Another 209 were excluded for psychosocial reasons (including 97 who withdrew voluntarily), and 173 for immunological reasons.
  - Perioperative: "sixteen donors (5.4%) suffered from complications including an iatrogenic pneumothorax (n = 1); hospital acquired infection (n = 3); and prolonged pain at the site of the nephrectomy scar (n = 11)." **One donor died of "significant haemorrhage in the immediate post-operative period"**. The other four deaths in the study period "were due to trauma and unrelated to donation." (A summarising fetch reported "no donation-related deaths". That is wrong: one death was donation-related.) Four donors (1.3%) needed psychological support after donation.
  - Long term: "no donor required kidney replacement therapy". eGFR <60 in 27% at a median of 3.7 years; albuminuria >300 mg/day in 4%; new-onset hypertension in 12.8%.
  - The authors conclude that the study "provides reassurance of the safety of the procedure in carefully selected donors in the developing world", and that "Published data on living donation in Africa is limited".
  - **Drafting cautions.** One death among 298 donors, over 34 years that include the open-surgery era, is a single event. It is not a rate to set against the US figures. The eGFR <60 figure measures kidney function after removing one kidney; it is not a disease diagnosis, so don't teach it as "27% got kidney disease". The Beginner Level-safe takeaway: **in South Africa most potential donors are turned away during assessment, and that screening is what keeps donation safe.** This supports the spec's case-by-case framing directly.
- **[SA, thesis, not peer-reviewed]** Murugan A, *A retrospective review with prospective follow up of renal function, blood pressure and proteinuria post living donor nephrectomy at Groote Schuur Hospital*, MMed thesis, University of Cape Town, 2020, hdl.handle.net/11427/32310. 154 living related donors, 2005–2017. Its abstract states: "There are no outcome data in Africa on long term donor renal function or mortality." Its follow-up denominators are very small (e.g. hypertension in 16 of 31). **Record it for completeness only; don't cite it in lesson prose.**

### 2.6 Living liver donor (partial liver): materially higher risk than kidney

- **[SA]** Botha J et al., "Living donor liver transplantation in South Africa: the donor experience," *S Afr J Surg* 2019;57(3):11–16, doi:10.17159/2078-5151/2019/v57n3a2998 (open access, SciELO SA). **Wits Donald Gordon Medical Centre, Johannesburg**, covering all 65 living-donor hepatectomies from March 2013 to December 2018. All were **left lateral segment** grafts (adult-to-child). Results:
  - **0 donor deaths (100% donor survival).**
  - Overall complication rate 30.7% (Clavien-Dindo), with 65% of complications grade I–II. Major complications 7% (5/65), including 2 grade IV and no grade V.
  - Median stay 7 days; no transfusions.
  - Donor limits applied: age ≤50, BMI <30, macrosteatosis ≤10%.
  - Two altruistic donors needed Ministerial/MAC approval, which corroborates §3.
  - The paper cites a donor-death estimate of 0.02–0.15% (Ratner & Sandoval, *Am J Transplant* 2010).
  - **Discrepancy:** the abstract gives a median operative time of 374 min, but the Results section gives 347 min (IQR 301–378).
- **[SA]** Calver KD et al., *SA J Radiol* 2024;28(1):2917: 117 SA living-donor liver transplants from March 2013 to August 2022, including 15 right-lobe grafts. **No published SA right-lobe donor-outcome series exists** (see Gaps).
- 📌 **[non-SA, worldwide survey]** Cheah YL et al., *Liver Transpl* 2013;19(5):499–506. 71 programmes in 21 countries, 11,553 donor hepatectomies: **donor mortality 0.2% (23/11,553)**, morbidity 24%, "near-miss" events 1.1%.
- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]** Muzaale AD et al., *Gastroenterology* 2012;142(2):273–280. US OPTN, 4,111 live liver donors: **early death 1.7 per 1,000**. Mortality at 11 years was 1.2% for liver donors, 1.2% for kidney donors and 1.4% for NHANES III (P=.9), so there was no excess long-term mortality.
- 📌 **[non-SA, Japan]** Hashikura Y et al., *Transplantation* 2009;88(1):110–114: 3,565 donors, 0.03% mortality, 8.4% complications. 📌 **[non-SA, Korea]** Lee JG et al., *Liver Transpl* 2017: 832 donors, 0 deaths, 1.9% major complications.
- 📌 **[non-SA, expert consensus]** ILTS guideline, Miller CM et al., *Transplantation* 2016;100(6):1238–1243: estimated donor mortality **0.1% for left-liver and 0.5% for right-liver** donors, with morbidity of 20–35%. **These are consensus estimates.** The Muzaale and Hashikura registries do not detect a right-lobe mortality gap. Present both views; don't smooth the split over.
- **Kidney vs liver.** Cite the two early-death rates separately: 📌 1.7 per 1,000 for liver (Muzaale 2012) and 📌 3.1 per 10,000 for kidney (Segev 2010). Don't compute a ratio, because no source states one.

## 3. The South African legal framework and donor workup for living donation

### 3.1 The statute: National Health Act 61 of 2003, Chapter 8 [SA]

Verbatim from the Act as gazetted (Gazette No. 26595, 23 July 2004):

- **s.1 definition**: "'tissue' means human tissue, and includes flesh, bone, a gland, an organ, skin, bone marrow or body fluid, but excludes blood or a gamete". *So the "tissue" sections below cover a living person giving a kidney or part of a liver.*
- **s.55**: "A person may not remove tissue, blood, a blood product or gametes from the body of another living person for the purpose referred to in section 56 unless it is done— (a) with the written consent of the person from whom the tissue, blood, blood product or gametes are removed granted in the prescribed manner; and (b) in accordance with prescribed conditions."
- **s.56(2)(a)**: the following may not be removed from a living person: "(i) Tissue, blood, a blood product or a gamete from a person who is mentally ill within the meaning of the Mental Health Care Act, 2002"; "(ii) tissue which is not replaceable by natural processes from a person younger than 18 years"; (iii) a gamete from a person under 18; (iv) placenta, embryonic or foetal tissue, stem cells and umbilical cord (excluding umbilical cord progenitor cells). **s.56(2)(b)**: "The Minister may authorise the removal or withdrawal of tissue, blood, a blood product or gametes contemplated in paragraph (a) and may impose any condition". *So a child cannot give a kidney or part of a liver without the Minister's authorisation.*
- **s.58(1)**: "A person may not remove tissue from a living person for transplantation in another living person or carry out the transplantation of such tissue except— (a) in a hospital or an authorised institution; and (b) on the written authority of— (i) the medical practitioner in charge of clinical services in that hospital or authorised institution, or any other medical practitioner authorised by him or her". **s.58(2)**: that practitioner "may not participate in a transplant for which he or she has granted authorisation". *So every living-donor transplant needs written sign-off from someone independent of the transplant team.* This is **in force for all living donors, related or not.** Thomson & Labuschaigne make the same reading: "Written medical manager authorisation is required in accordance with section 58 of the National Health Act for all living donor transplants" (*S Afr J Bioethics Law* 2024;17(1):e1561 **[SA, academic]**).
- **s.60(4)**: "It is an offence for a person— (a) who has donated tissue, a gamete, blood or a blood product to receive any form of financial or other reward for such donation, except for the reimbursement of reasonable costs incurred by him or her to provide such donation; and (b) to sell or trade in tissue, gametes, blood or blood products, except as provided for in this Chapter." **s.60(5)**: on conviction, "a fine or to imprisonment for a period not exceeding five years or to both".
- **s.61(3)**: "An organ may not be transplanted into a person who is not a South African citizen or a permanent resident of the Republic without the Minister's authorisation in writing." **This is the only ministerial authorisation in s.61, and it concerns non-citizen recipients, not unrelated donors.** s.61(4) requires the Minister to prescribe criteria for approving organ transplant facilities. s.61(5) makes charging a fee for a human organ an offence (up to 5 years).

### 3.2 The 2012 regulations: GN R180 [SA]

**GN R180 of 2 March 2012** (Government Gazette No. 35099) is the *Regulations Regarding the General Control of Human Bodies, Tissue, Blood, Blood Products and Gametes*, made under s.90(1) read with s.68(1) of the Act. On living donors it provides:
- **reg 2**: written consent, given by the person themselves if older than 18, or by their parents or guardians if younger than 18;
- **reg 3(1)**: tissue from living persons "may only be used for medical and dental purposes", including "the transplanting thereof in the body of another living person";
- **reg 4**: the permitted donees (hospital, university, authorised institution, medical practitioner, dentist, tissue bank, or a person who needs the therapy).

Offences under **reg 25** carry "a fine or imprisonment for a period of 10 years or to both". **R180 contains no requirement for ministerial approval of unrelated living donors.** Its reg 9 is the two-doctor death-certification rule.

Gazette 35099 actually carries **nine** Chapter 8 notices (R175–R183): artificial fertilisation; clinical forensic medicine; use of human biological material; microbiological laboratories; blood and blood products; R180; import and export; tissue banks; and stem cell banks. A sub-agent extracted the text of all nine. **"Organ transplant" appears in none of them, and none has a related/unrelated test for living donors.**

R180 reg 27 repeals GN R2876 of 29 December 1989 (a Human Tissue Act-era instrument). The Act's Schedule repeals the Human Tissue Act 65 of 1983 entirely. *Unverified guess:* the pre-2012 unrelated-donor rule may have sat in GN R2876/1989. Its text could not be found online.

SA academic commentary agrees. McQuoid-Mason D, "Human tissue and organ transplant provisions: Chapter 8 of the National Health Act and its Regulations, in effect from March 2012 – what doctors must know," *S Afr Med J* 2012;102(9), PMID 22958690 **[SA, peer-reviewed commentary]**, identifies R180 as the governing regulation. It reads ministerial written authorisation as applying to non-citizen recipients.

### 3.3 Unrelated living donors need the Minister's approval (MACOT) in practice, but no regulation in force requires it yet [SA]

Three current SA documents state this as a firm requirement:
- Barday ZA, McCurdie F, du Toit T, Wearne N, "A kidney exchange programme for South Africa – the time is right," *S Afr Med J* 2025;115(9):e4081, doi:10.7196/SAMJ.2025.v115i9.4081 (editorial, open access): "Because of concerns around organ trafficking, in SA all living donor transplants between unrelated DRPs must be approved by the National Minister of Health, who is assisted and advised by a ministerial advisory committee for organ transplants (MACOT)."
- GSH KPD SOP (23 May 2025, via SATS), §3: "National Health Act (No. 61 of 2003, Chapter 8) regulates the legal framework for living organ donation. Within this legislative context, all living donations require formal approval through provincial structures to ensure ethical and medical oversight. In addition, all living unrelated transplants require approval by the National Minister of Health, who is advised by the Ministerial Advisory Committee for Organ Transplants (MACOT)." Annexure I §3 adds that donor and recipient must be "approved for transplantation by their local transplant panel and the Minister of Health (via the Ministerial Advisory Committee for Organ Transplants)".
- SATS/SANS joint position statement, *A National Kidney Exchange Program* (Walabh, SATS President; Wearne, SANS President; via sats.org.za/position-statements/): "incompatible DRPs need to have MACOT approval before they can be referred for entry onto the program."

The same requirement also appears in older practice: Botha 2019 (§2.6) reports that two altruistic liver donors needed Ministerial/MAC approval.

**Who counts as "unrelated"?** Red File pp.14–15 **[SA]** lists three types of living donor:
- a **related living donor**, "such as a parent, sister, brother, or grandparent";
- a **non-related living donor**, "a person known, but not related to the recipient, such as a husband, wife, or family friend";
- an **altruistic donor**, "a person who does not know the recipient and volunteers to donate an organ as a selfless act of kindness".

**So a spouse counts as a non-related donor. There is no spousal exception.**

**⚠️ No legal basis in force was found for the unrelated-donor approval rule.**
- In the Act, the Minister's authorisation appears only in s.56(2)(b) (minors, the mentally ill and the other barred categories) and s.61(3) (non-citizen recipients).
- None of the nine 2012 notices contains it (§3.2).
- A 2013 SA law-journal article already noted the missing instrument: "If a person is unrelated ministerial permission must be obtained for the transplantation"; "Currently, there are no specific regulations for transplants published, yet these regulations are still under discussion between role-players such as the government and the South African Transplant Society" (Venter & Slabbert, "Rewarding a living kidney donor: a comparison between South Africa, Singapore and Iran", *Obiter* 2013, pp.189–190 and fn 27 **[SA, academic]**).
- **A search for unpublished ministerial directives or circulars was not done, so one can't be ruled out.**

**The rule is written down only in a draft published this month. [SA, DRAFT, NOT IN FORCE]**
- **Instrument:** *Regulations on Organ Transplantation*, Department of Health, Government Notice No. 7879, **Government Gazette No. 55299, 4 September 2026**, pp.72–95, gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202609/55299gon7879.pdf. Short title (reg 28): "the Regulations on Transplantation, 2026".
- **Status:** a draft for comment. The cover page reads "The Minister of Health intends, in terms of section 68(1)(r) of the National Health Act … to make the Regulations in the Schedule". Comments are due "within three months of this Notice", so the window closes around early December 2026.
- **Reg 10(2)**, verified on the scan: "No person may remove an organ from a living person for transplant into another person without the Minister's written approval unless the person into whom the organ is to be transplanted is biologically related to the person from whom the organ is removed."
- **Reg 10(3)**, who counts as "biologically related":
  - (a) "natural parents and children";
  - (b) "brother and sisters sharing one or both natural parents";
  - (c) "the brother and sister of the natural parent";
  - (d) "the natural children of the natural brothers and sisters of the natural parents";
  - (e) "natural children of natural brothers and sisters";
  - (f) "Biologically related grandparents and grandchildren."
- **Reg 10(4)** requires "adequate proof of the claimed relationship".
- **Reg 11**, "Procedure for application for Ministerial approval for local living unrelated": "the Minister may grant permission for the transplant, on receipt of an application and documentation detailed in Annexure B".
- **Reg 10(1)(c)–(f)**, verified on the scan: the institution must "ensure and document that the donor is willing to donate, free from inducement and coercion"; "There is no financial and non-financial incentive to donate"; and "The donor has been informed that he or she may, without prejudice, withdraw the donation at any time."
- **Annexure A**, sub-agent reading:
  - "Unrelated donors may include but not be limited to spouses, friends and acquaintances";
  - applications "must be recommended by the Ministerial Advisory Committee (MAC) or another committee established for this purpose" and "approved or disapproved by the Minister";
  - an independent psychological assessment is required;
  - "Living donors must be aged 18 years or older".
- **Reg 27** (penalty): up to ten years.

**How to teach it:**
- The requirement is **real in practice**: SA clinicians and SATS apply it, and SA transplant units have operated under it since at least 2013 (Botha 2019).
- It is **not yet in a regulation in force.** The draft of 4 September 2026 would codify it.
- **Don't write "the law requires ministerial approval for unrelated donors."** Write that SA practice requires the Health Minister's approval, advised by an expert committee, and that draft regulations to formalise this were published for comment in September 2026.
- Re-check before lesson drafting in case the regulations have been promulgated.
- Both current practice and the draft agree that **spouses count as unrelated**.

### 3.4 What a living donor goes through in SA: the workup [SA]

From the GSH KPD SOP (2025) and its annexures, via SATS:
- "Routine transplant work-up for clinical suitability, including blood group and tissue typing, is undertaken as for any living donor kidney transplant."
- "a further formal social worker assessment and, where deemed necessary, a structured psychological evaluation to assess emotional readiness, capacity for informed consent, understanding of the KPD process, and absence of coercion."
- "Potential donors and recipients receive separate counselling sessions, so that each person can make up their own mind in their own time."
- A donor may withdraw "up to the point of being put to sleep (going under general anaesthetic) for the donor operation."
- Money (Annexure C §4): "Any payment between donors and recipients either directly or indirectly is illegal under South African law … All donors need to be donating freely and with no bullying or bribing. This will be looked for and is punishable by law." Also: "additional costs, such as transportation or loss of income, will not be covered."

From the SATS *Position Statement Social Media Donor 2025* **[SA]**:
- Unrelated and altruistic donors go through "the standard ULD [unrelated living donor] workup process and approvals framework".
- A local "Transplant Panel meeting for unanimous decision to proceed or not to proceed" comes before the ministerial committee.
- Psychosocial screening covers "potential financial motivation to donate".
- "At any stage of the assessment if candidates are found to be unsuitable the process will be halted".

From Dayal 2022 **[SA]**: at Charlotte Maxeke, "Donor evaluation at this facility is in accordance with existing KDIGO guidelines". Pre-donation GFR was measured with chromium-51-EDTA scans.

## 4. Myth #11: "Past illness or a cancer history excludes you from donating"

### 4.1 What ODF and SATCS say [SA]

- **ODF FAQ** (verbatim):
  - "Who can be an organ / tissue donor?" "Any person who is in good health and is clear of defined chronic diseases that might adversely affect the recipient will be considered as a possible donor."
  - "Can I be a donor if I have an existing medical condition?" "Having a medical condition does not necessarily prevent a person from becoming an organ / tissue donor. The decision about which organs / tissue will be transplanted will be established at your time of death."
  - "Do I need medical tests to register as a donor?" "No. Medical tests will only be carried out at the time of death. This will involve medical professionals evaluating your medical and social history, carrying out blood and culture tests and conducting a physical examination."
  - **This grounds the spec's myth-#11 framing directly: not absolute, assessed at the time of death, by clinicians, on the patient's profile.** ODF says nothing specific about cancer.
- **Red File p.20 (SATCS)**:
  - "There is no age restriction for organ donation."
  - "The transplant coordinator and transplant team - who are aware of urgent listings (patients who may accept marginal organs) and are up to date on the latest medical advances in organ recovery - will discuss and document donor suitability." *This is the "experienced clinicians" part of the spec's framing, in SA words.*
  - "When in doubt about donor suitability, please refer."
  - The Red File's list of causes of brain death in potential organ donors includes "primary brain tumour". *A cancer can be the cause of death of an organ donor.*

### 4.2 "Extended criteria donors": T01's Transplant Alchemy claim, corroborated and corrected

- **The claim traces to an SA primary source.** Red File p.20 (SATCS) **[SA]**, verbatim: "To address donor scarcity, extended selection criteria are sometimes applied and patients with certain comorbidities are considered as donors. Extended criteria donors have shown excellent outcomes and offer a significant survival benefit over no transplant. Individual organs are assessed for their transplantability in a potential donor with comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, and HIV. PLEASE REFER ALL POTENTIAL DONORS."
- **T01 quoted Transplant Alchemy 101 Objective 5 cut short.** The full sentence reads: "Extended criteria donors with comorbidities like hypertension, diabetes, or HIV may still be eligible **if individual organs are assessed as transplantable**." The dropped condition is the case-by-case point, so any lesson copy must keep it. Transplant Alchemy is a near-paraphrase of the Red File, so **cite the Red File, not the build's study guide.**
- 📌 **[non-SA, USA] The technical term means something narrower.** Port FK et al., "Donor characteristics associated with reduced graft survival: an approach to expanding the pool of kidney donors," *Transplantation* 2002;74(9):1281–1286: "Expanded donor kidneys… included all donors aged 60 years and older and those aged 50 to 59 years with at least two of the other three conditions (cerebrovascular cause of death, renal insufficiency [serum creatinine >1.5 mg/dL], hypertension)." The definition applies to kidneys only. US allocation used it from 31 October 2002, and the OPTN/SRTR 2014 Annual Data Report (Hart et al., *Am J Transplant* 2016, doi:10.1111/ajt.13666) records its replacement in December 2014 by the Kidney Donor Profile Index. The KDPI scores donor age, height, weight, race/ethnicity, "history of hypertension or diabetes", cause of death, creatinine, hepatitis C status and DCD status.
- **Verdict.**
  - **The claim is correct as a statement of SA practice**, because SATCS says it in almost those words.
  - **It is loose as terminology.** Diabetes is not in the strict ECD definition. HIV has nothing to do with ECD; HIV-positive donation runs as its own programme (§4.3).
  - For lesson copy, **don't use "extended criteria donor" as a technical label**, and don't attach 📌 US definitions to it. Say, citing the Red File: donors with conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes or HIV can still be considered, because each organ is assessed individually.

### 4.3 HIV: a South African first

- **[SA] HIV-positive to HIV-positive kidney transplantation began at Groote Schuur Hospital in 2008.**
  - Muller E, Kahn D, Mendelson M, *N Engl J Med* 2010;362(24):2336–2337, doi:10.1056/NEJMc0900837. Four transplants "from September through November 2008". At 12 months, "All patients had good renal function, did not have clinically significant graft rejection, and have not needed dialysis since the procedure."
  - Muller E, Barday Z, Mendelson M, Kahn D, "HIV-positive-to-HIV-positive kidney transplantation — results at 3 to 5 years," *N Engl J Med* 2015;372(7):613–620, doi:10.1056/NEJMoa1408896 (University of Cape Town / Groote Schuur Hospital). "From September 2008 through February 2014, a total of 27 HIV-positive patients underwent kidney transplantation." "The rate of survival among the patients was 84% at 1 year, 84% at 3 years, and 74% at 5 years. The corresponding rates of graft survival were 93%, 84%, and 84%." Kidneys came from "deceased donors who tested positive for HIV". Recipients were on antiretroviral therapy, with CD4 ≥200 and an undetectable viral load.
- **[SA] A living HIV-positive donor.** Botha J et al., *AIDS* 2018;32(16):F13–F19, PMID 30281558. In 2017 an HIV-positive mother gave part of her liver to her HIV-negative child at Wits Donald Gordon, Johannesburg: "No plasma, or cell-associated HIV-1 DNA has been detected in the recipient." This was a single, exceptional case, **not a general pathway.**
- **[SA] A national clinical guideline exists.** Nel JS, Conradie F, Botha J, Etheredge H, Fabian J, Levin L, Mazanderani AH, Moorhouse M, Muller E, Tiemessen C, Thomson D, Turner J, "Southern African HIV Clinicians Society guidelines for solid organ transplantation in human immunodeficiency virus: An evidence-based framework for human immunodeficiency virus-positive donors and recipients," *South Afr J HIV Med* 2020;21(1):1133, doi:10.4102/sajhivmed.v21i1.1133. The panel included representatives of SATS and the NICD. On deceased HIV-positive donors: "Standard criteria, as for HIV-negative deceased donors." On viral load: donors need not be virally suppressed, because "limiting the deceased donor pool only to virally suppressed individuals would significantly restrict the number of organs available." **This HIV guideline is the only SA donor-suitability guideline found. It says nothing on cancer or hepatitis.**
- **[SA] Living kidney donors with HIV were excluded** at Charlotte Maxeke, 1981–2015. HIV was among the commonest medical reasons for exclusion (Dayal 2022: 44 of 910 excluded donors).
- 📌 **[non-SA] Context.**
  - US: the HOPE Act final rule, 89 FR 93484 (27 Nov 2024), removed the research-only requirement for HIV-positive-to-HIV-positive kidney and liver transplants.
  - UK: NHSBT POL188 lists "HIV disease (not HIV infection only)" as an absolute contraindication.
  - **Groote Schuur's 2008 programme came first, and SA practice is its own primary source**, so no 📌 is needed for the SA claim.

### 4.4 Cancer history: the clinical position

- **[SA] No SA national guideline on donors with a cancer history was found.**
  - The SATS resources page has none.
  - The SATS *Organ and Tissue Donation in South Africa – Strategic Roadmap* (report of a September 2019 workshop, uploaded 2024) lists only the SA death-determination guidelines and the SAHCS HIV guideline as existing resources. It names "Inclusion and exclusion criteria" as a training need.
  - **The SA-sourced facts on cancer are therefore limited to:** the Red File's tissue rules (§5); a primary brain tumour listed as a cause of death in organ donors (Red File p.20); and "please refer" (Red File p.20).
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK] Five-tier transmission risk.** SaBTO (Advisory Committee on the Safety of Blood, Tissues and Organs), *Transplantation of organs from deceased donors with cancer or a history of cancer*, v2.1 (first published April 2014, revised December 2020). gov.uk still shows it as current, and NHSBT POL188/17 (effective 30 Jan 2024) cites it. **It is past its own stated review date** ("To be revised no later than November 2023").
  - **Tiers (p.5), verbatim:**
    - "Absolute contra-indication"
    - "High risk (estimated at >10%)"
    - "Intermediate risk (estimated at between 2% and 10%)"
    - "Low risk (estimated at between 0.1% and 2%)"
    - "Minimal risk (estimated at <0.1%)"

    It then says: "these categories are somewhat arbitrary… there is no category of zero risk."
  - **Absolute contraindications** (Table 2): "Active cancer with spread outside the organ"; "Active haematological malignancy". Brain and spinal cord tumours (Table 1): "Primary cerebral lymphoma", "All secondary intracranial tumours", "Any cancer with metastatic spread".
  - **Minimal-risk examples** (<0.1%):
    - "Skin: basal cell carcinoma"
    - "Skin: squamous cell carcinoma with no metastases"
    - non-melanoma skin cancer in situ
    - "Uterine Cervix: in situ cancer"
    - low-grade bladder cancer (pTa G1/G2)
    - renal cell cancer "<1.0cm"
    - small thyroid cancers
    - "Prostate: Gleason <6, or <7 with curative treatment and cancer free >5 years"
  - **Disease-free intervals** (low-risk tier):
    - breast stage 1A, cancer-free ">10 years" (intermediate at 5–10 years);
    - colon stage 1A, cancer-free ">5 years";
    - melanoma pT1a (<0.8mm), cancer-free ">5 years";
    - renal cell cancer 1–4cm, cancer-free ">3 years".
  - **Cancers not in the tables:** "Cancers not included in this guidance must be considered on a case by case basis following appropriate professional consultation."
  - **Corrections to common assumptions.** In SaBTO, melanoma is "High risk", not an automatic exclusion. NHSBT POL188/17 lists melanoma as absolute, with an exception: over 5 years ago, no nodal spread, Breslow depth <0.8mm, and dermato-oncology review. Haematological malignancy is absolute only when *active*. Brain tumours are graded by tier, not excluded wholesale.
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK] How often cancer actually passes to a recipient.**
  - SaBTO recommendation 3: "The risk of transmission of a cancer to a recipient in the UK from a donor not previously known to have a cancer is around 0.05% (1 in 2000)". The source is Desai R et al., *Transplantation* 2012;94(12):1200–1207: 15 transmissions among 30,765 transplants from 14,986 donors.
  - Desai R et al., *Br J Surg* 2014;101(7):768–774: 17,639 UK donors, 202 with a cancer history, 61 classed unacceptable or high risk. "No cancer transmission was noted in 133 recipients of organs from these 61 donors." The authors add: "Strict implementation of present guidelines is likely to result in overestimation of cancer transmission risk in some donors."
  - Watson CJE et al., *Am J Transplant* 2010;10(6):1437–1444: 448 recipients of organs from 177 donors with primary brain cancer. "No transmission of donor intracranial malignancy occurred."
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK] The guidelines' own risk-benefit framing** (the non-SA anchor for "case by case"):
  - SaBTO recommendation 2: "The risk of inadvertent cancer transmission must be balanced against the risk of a patient dying waiting for a transplant or becoming too sick for the procedure to be successful if the organs are not used."
  - NHSBT POL188/17, p.1: "Organs from any donor will carry some degree of risk and the risks associated with transplantation must be balanced against the benefits of transplantation and the risks of remaining on the waiting list"; "Localised prostate, thyroid, in situ cervical cancer and non-melanotic skin cancers are acceptable as possible organ donors."
- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]** Kaul DR et al., "Ten years of donor-derived disease: A report of the disease transmission advisory committee," *Am J Transplant* 2021;21(2):689–702, doi:10.1111/ajt.16178: "Unexpected DDD was rare, occurring in 0.18% of all transplant recipients." Malignancies made up 29% of proven or probable events. **0.18% covers all donor-derived disease, not cancer only; don't derive a cancer-only rate from it.** The US DTAC risk bands (Nalesnik et al., *Am J Transplant* 2011) differ from SaBTO's, so never mix the two schemes.

### 4.5 Hepatitis B and C

- **[SA]** Nothing on solid-organ donors with hepatitis was found in an SA source. The Red File and the ODF Cornea FAQ exclude hepatitis B and C for all tissue and cornea donation (§5).
- 📌 **[non-SA, UK]** SaBTO, *Microbiological safety guidelines*, v2.2 (revised July 2023):
  - 12.17.6: "HCV infection in the potential donor does not amount to an absolute contra-indication to donation of material for life-preserving transplantation… This risk/benefit analysis allows for the potential use of a transplant from a HCV infected donor to a non-infected recipient."
  - Table 7: HBsAg-positive is a "Relative contraindication" for organs; "Anti-HBc positive, HBsAg negative": "Donation permitted".
- 📌 **[non-SA, USA]** Jones JM et al., "Assessing Solid Organ Donors and Monitoring Transplant Recipients for HIV, HBV, and HCV Infection — U.S. Public Health Service Guideline, 2020," *MMWR Recomm Rep* 2020;69(4):1–16. It retired the "increased risk donor" label.

## 5. Tissue and cornea eligibility differs from solid-organ eligibility [SA]

From the SATCS Red File. Printed page numbers are given for each rule.

| Tissue | Age window | Cancer rule | Infection rule | Other exclusions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cornea / eye** (pp.21, 41) | 6–65 years, "and even up to 70 years in certain cases" | Death from "most cancers" is **acceptable**, because "Corneas do not contain blood vessels, eliminating the risk of transmitting most types of cancer". **Lymphoma and leukaemia exclude.** | TB, syphilis, hepatitis B and C, HIV/AIDS, and any untreated systemic infection exclude | Previous laser eye surgery; corneal scarring; glaucoma; keratoconus; unknown cause of death. "Cataracts and poor eyesight do not prevent cornea donation." |
| **Bone, tendon, ligament** (p.42) | 16–80 years | **Cancer excludes** | Septicaemia, hepatitis B and/or C, syphilis and HIV/AIDS exclude | Unknown cause of death |
| **Skin** (p.42) | 16–80 years | **Cancer excludes** | As for bone | Prolonged steroid therapy; chronic skin disease; psoriasis; unknown cause of death |
| **Heart valves** (p.42) | 6 months up to and including 55 years | **Cancer excludes** | As for bone, plus blood-borne infections | Poisoning; trauma to the heart; unknown cause of death |
| **Living: femoral head** (p.43) | 18–80, primary hip replacement patients | **Cancer excludes** | Hepatitis B and/or C, syphilis and HIV/AIDS exclude | — |

Two further SA sources on the same point:
- **ODF Cornea FAQ**, "Who can be a cornea donor?": "Anyone can be a donor… The exclusions are as follows: positive virology results for infectious diseases such as HIV/AIDS, Hepatitis B or C and Septicemia." The ODF version omits the lymphoma, leukaemia and laser-surgery exclusions that the Red File lists.
- **ODF Cornea FAQ**: "Less than 1% of corneas previously donated to the bank have been deemed unsuitable for transplant." This is the Eye Bank's own figure.

**The teachable contrast.** The answer to "does cancer exclude me?" depends on *what* is being donated.
- A history of most cancers does not stop cornea donation.
- It does exclude skin, bone and heart-valve donation.
- A primary brain tumour can be the cause of death of an organ donor (Red File p.20).
- HIV is assessed organ by organ for solid organs (Red File p.20), but excludes all tissue and cornea donation.

This is the SA-sourced core for myth #11: **not absolute, it depends on the organ or tissue, and a coordinator assesses it.**

**Why tissue rules are stricter.** No SA source states the reasoning. The clearest primary statement is 📌 **[non-SA, UK]** SaBTO, *Microbiological safety guidelines* v2.2 (July 2023), §3(c):
> "the risk of infection from a particular donor may be an absolute contra-indication to accepting a bone donation but only a relative contra-indication for liver donation where the potential recipient might otherwise die from liver failure. For this reason, the criteria used to accept a tissue donor are stricter than those for organ donors."

The same document, §1.6: "In urgent life-preserving situations, a higher risk of infection may be acceptable whilst stricter controls are needed… for transplants aimed at improving a patient's quality of life rather than saving it."

Its Tables 7–9 set the two side by side. HIV and HCV are "Relative contraindication" for organs but "Absolute contraindication" for tissues. For HBsAg, organs are "Relative contraindication" and tissues "Contraindication". NHSBT POL188/17 excludes all tissue donation for "History of Haematological malignancy" and "History of chronic viral hepatitis or HIV infection", and multi-tissue donation for any malignancy except basal cell carcinoma and carcinoma in situ.

The SA Red File applies the same logic without spelling it out: HIV is assessed organ by organ for solid organs (p.20) but excludes every tissue (pp.41–43). **Teach the reasoning with 📌 and the rules from the Red File.**

## 6. Drafting notes for Stage 2

- **Myth #12, SA-grounded core.**
  - Living donation is common in SA. In 2021 there were 57 living-donor kidney and 33 living-donor liver transplants (SATS 5-year report).
  - Every donor goes through a medical, blood-group and tissue-typing, social-work and (where needed) psychological assessment (GSH SOP).
  - SA clinicians call the kidney operation "safe and low-risk" but not risk-free (GSH brochure).
  - Most people who come forward are turned away at assessment (Dayal 2022: fewer than a quarter went on to donate).
  - Every living-donor transplant needs written authority from the hospital's clinical head, who may not take part in it (NHA s.58, in force).
  - Unrelated donors, spouses included, need the Minister's approval on an expert committee's advice (practice; the draft regulations were published September 2026).
  - Paying a donor is a crime (NHA s.60(4)).
- **Myth #12, numbers.**
  - Every number in lesson prose carries 📌. Kidney (US): "around 3 in 10,000 historically, now under 1 in 10,000". Liver (worldwide survey): "about 1 in 500".
  - Long term, kidney donors' lifetime kidney-failure risk is higher than for equally healthy people but lower than the general population's (📌 Muzaale 2014).
  - The one SA liver series had no donor deaths among 65 donors (Botha 2019, SA).
- **Myth #11, SA-grounded core.**
  - "Having a medical condition does not necessarily prevent a person from becoming an organ / tissue donor" (ODF).
  - Suitability is decided at the time of death (ODF) by the transplant coordinator and team (Red File).
  - Donors with high blood pressure, diabetes or HIV are assessed organ by organ (Red File).
  - South Africa pioneered transplanting kidneys from HIV-positive donors into HIV-positive recipients (Groote Schuur, 2008; NEJM 2010, 2015).
  - Whether cancer rules you out depends on what is donated (Red File table, §5).
  - The 📌 UK guidance classes cancers from minimal to unacceptable risk, and says cancers outside its tables are considered "case by case".
- **Quiz-safe SA facts** (no 📌 needed):
  - Corneas can be donated even if you wear glasses or have cataracts.
  - Most cancers don't stop cornea donation, but leukaemia and lymphoma do.
  - HIV excludes tissue and cornea donation, but not necessarily organ donation.
  - A spouse counts as an unrelated living donor.
  - SA has no age limit for organ donation.
  - Tissue can be donated up to days after death, and an open-casket funeral is still possible.
- **Quiz traps to avoid:**
  - Don't make "3 in 10,000" a quiz answer without 📌 and the historical framing.
  - Don't present "extended criteria donor" as the UNOS technical category.
  - Don't claim ministerial approval of unrelated donors is written into the Act or a regulation in force.
  - Don't use "perfect body" as a quotation.
  - Don't say donation shortens, or doesn't shorten, life expectancy.
  - Don't say melanoma is always an absolute exclusion; UK sources disagree.
  - Don't quote Dayal's "27% eGFR <60" as "27% got kidney disease".

## 7. Flagged gaps

- **G7a — No SA living-donor mortality rate, and no SA national living-donor registry.** Dayal 2022 (one centre, 298 donors) and Botha 2019 (one centre, 65 liver donors) are the only peer-reviewed SA donor-outcome series found. Every rate is 📌 non-SA.
- **G7b — No SA right-lobe living liver donor outcome data.** SA has done at least 15 right-lobe grafts (Calver 2024), but no donor-outcome series has been published.
- **G7c — The unrelated-donor approval rule has no instrument in force.**
  - No such rule appears in the Act, in any of the nine 2012 notices, or in anything else published; unpublished directives were not searched.
  - It is codified only in the **draft** *Regulations on Organ Transplantation* (GN 7879, GG 55299, 4 Sept 2026), whose comment period closes around early December 2026.
  - **Re-check whether they have been promulgated before Stage 2 is drafted.** If promulgated, cite the final regulation number and drop the "practice" hedge.
  - The draft names the committee "MAC"; practice documents say "MACOT". The draft neither creates the committee nor sets its membership.
- **G7d — ODF says nothing on living-donor safety or on cancer history.** SATS's only donor-safety statement sits inside a hospital SOP. Its headline number (3.1/10,000, "not changed for the last 15 years") is out of date against Massie 2024. SATS may want to know.
- **G7e — No SA guideline on donors with a cancer history.** The only SA donor-suitability guideline is the 2020 SAHCS HIV guideline. Every solid-organ cancer rule in this file is 📌 UK (SaBTO/NHSBT). SaBTO's cancer guidance is itself past its own review date (Nov 2023). Nothing on solid-organ donors with hepatitis was found in an SA source either.
- **G7f — The EDQM/Council of Europe organ guide could not be read** (bot challenge on the PDF). Its tier names and bands are unconfirmed, and SaBTO is the only non-SA tiered scheme verified here. The US DTAC bands (Nalesnik 2011) were seen only secondhand and differ from SaBTO's.
- **G7g — Figure 17 of the SATS 5-year report is internally inconsistent**: the living-donation totals exceed living kidney plus living liver. This is unexplained; the per-organ figures are used here.
- **G3 — closed.** The Tissue Bank FAQ does *not* carry myth #10's content; it now cites the ODF main FAQ, the ODF Cornea FAQ and the Red File instead (§1). The spec's Open Items entry "Tissue Bank FAQ direct review" can be struck. The "perfect body" wording has no source.
