# Intermediate Level Research Findings

Grounds the **Intermediate Level** (donation process, ethics, South African legal framework; audience: general public + healthcare-adjacent) against the Source Corpus and current (2026) South African primary sources, per ticket T02.

Method: primary Source Corpus PDFs read directly (page ranges noted per source); current legal/regulatory status verified via web search against government-gazette-level sources where possible, with secondary journalism used only for framing/context and flagged as such.

## 1. Process topics: referral, brain death determination, consent, allocation

### 1.1 Referral
- There is **no legal requirement to refer** a potential donor, but SATCS best practice is "refer every death" for tissue and "refer all potential organ donors" before death where possible, so end-of-life discussions can be fully informed. Referral responsibility sits with the **entire treating team** (medical, nursing, allied), not only the treating physician. — *Document H – SATS Red File*, pp.18, 20, 22.
- Clinical triggers for referral to a transplant coordinator: (1) decision made to perform brain-death testing; (2) catastrophic brain injury with GCS ≤4 not explained by sedation; (3) decision to withdraw life-sustaining treatment with death expected. — *Document H*, p.17 (§2.4), corroborated in the Western Cape provincial policy circular H84/2025 embedded in the course manual, p.4 (§5).
- Best practice: the transplant-coordinator discussion should happen **before** end-of-life conversations with family, to screen for feasibility, check the national priority list, and check Organ Donor Foundation (ODF) registration status — this "decoupling" avoids a premature or false donation conversation. — *Document H*, p.5 (Wits FACTS excerpt) and course manual Western Cape circular, p.5.
- Long-distance referral protocol (what information a transplant coordinator needs) is documented step-by-step. — *Document H*, pp.35–36.

### 1.2 Brain death (neurological) and circulatory death determination
- **Primary authoritative source: South African Guidelines on the Determination of Death**, Critical Care Society of Southern Africa (CCSSA), published (peer-reviewed) as *S Afr Med J* 2021;111(4b):367-380 and *S Afr J Crit Care* 2021;37(1b):41-54, DOI 10.7196/SAJCC.2021v37i1b.466 — full text read as `SAJCC-37-1-466.pdf` (all 14 pages).
  - Death is determined by **either neurological criteria (brain death) or circulatory criteria**; both are legally and clinically "death," not "life support withdrawal."
  - Brain-death testing requires an established, irreversible cause; preconditions (temperature >36 °C, systolic BP >100 mmHg or MAP >60 mmHg, exclusion of CNS-depressant drug effect, intact neuromuscular function, correction of severe metabolic/endocrine derangement).
  - Testing = (1) coma, (2) absent brainstem reflexes (pupillary, corneal, trigeminal pain, vestibulo-ocular/cold-caloric, gag, cough), (3) apnoea test (target paCO₂ >60 mmHg / 8 kPa, pH <7.30).
  - **Two doctors** must confirm brain death; one must have ≥5 years' registration since qualifying; **neither may be part of the transplant team**. Circulatory-death cases involving organ donation likewise require a second, independent doctor.
  - Paediatric (cannot diagnose <36 weeks corrected gestation), ECMO, and pregnancy-specific provisions are included.
  - Circulatory death: no CPR indicated / CPR failed / treatment withdrawn; 5-minute observation of absent circulation/respiration, then confirm absent pupillary/motor response.
  - Family "accommodation" (brief additional time before withdrawing somatic support) is addressed as an ethical/practical concern, capped ordinarily at 24 hours.
  - Standardised checklists (brain death, circulatory death, apnoea testing) are provided and are also the ones referenced by the Western Cape provincial policy and the course manual as "Appendix 4."
- **Note on corpus duplication:** `South-African-Guidelines-on-the-Determination-of-Death-CCSSA-Website-Version.pdf` is a **pre-publication DRAFT** (watermarked "DRAFT," with an incomplete placeholder table of endorsing societies). Content is materially identical to the published SAJCC version but the SAJCC file is the authoritative, citable, peer-reviewed one — see Currency Notes §5.
- `Document H – SATS Red File` reproduces a simplified bedside version of the same criteria (pp.23–28), including an **atropine test** presented as an optional "coordinator-specific" test not required for certification — this is a SATCS addition not found in the CCSSA/SAJCC guideline itself; flagged so course content doesn't present it as a universal legal requirement.

### 1.3 Consent
- Consent for organ donation must be obtained, in the legally defined order of preference, from the **next of kin or legal guardian** (spouse/partner > major child > parent/guardian > major brother or sister), plus **forensic pathologist/district surgeon consent** in unnatural-death (medico-legal) cases, plus **hospital manager/medical-superintendent authorisation**. — *Document H*, pp.18–19; corroborated by the 2012 SAMJ legal explainer (McQuoid-Mason, "Human tissue and organ transplant provisions: Chapter 8 of the National Health Act and its Regulations," *S Afr Med J* 2012;102(9):733-735), reproduced in the course manual.
- Consent must be **in writing or telephonically**, witnessed by two people for deceased organ/tissue donation.
- Family-conversation practice is governed in the corpus by the **Wits Transplant "FACTS" protocol** (Family Approach to Consent for Transplant Strategy), an 8-step adaptation of the UK NHS Blood and Transplant "Approaching the Families of Potential Organ Donors" guidance for the South African setting — reproduced in *Document H*, pp.38+. Key ethical features: decoupling death-notification from the donation ask, assessing family understanding/acceptance of death first, giving the family private time to decide, and continued support regardless of outcome.
- Registering as a donor with the **Organ Donor Foundation (ODF)** records *intent* only — it does **not** substitute for next-of-kin consent at time of death; the ODF explicitly states registration "does not mean that the donor's organs will automatically be donated at the time of death" and next-of-kin consent is always still required. — *Document H*, p.11. The ODF is also explicitly **not** a medical or allocation body — it does public awareness, education, and registry maintenance only (p.8).

### 1.4 Allocation
- Corpus coverage is thin. `Document H` notes only that "donated organs are allocated to patients who are on transplant waiting lists at hospitals nationally" (p.14) and that transplant coordinators check the "national priority listing" and consider "extended criteria" donors (patients with comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, HIV) to expand the donor pool (p.20).
- No document in the corpus lays out the actual allocation algorithm/matching criteria (e.g., blood type, tissue match, waiting time weighting, geographic rules). **This is a gap** — see §6.
- Current scale, for context (secondary/statistical source, not legal): SATS-reported 2021 data show ~2,586 people on the national waiting list (2,382 for kidney, 108 heart, 52 liver, 44 lung) against only 229 transplants performed that year, across 21 transplant centres nationally (14 kidney, 6 heart, 4 lung, 4 liver, 1 pancreas). — SATS *National Strategic Roadmap*, Sept 2024, and *Spotlight* (29 Sept 2025), "SA has very low organ donation rates – how can we fix it?" (secondary journalism, useful for framing scarcity but not a legal/clinical source).

### 1.5 DBD vs DCD pathway framework
- The critical-pathway model (possible donor → potential donor → eligible donor → actual donor → utilised donor), adapted from Domínguez-Gil et al. 2011 (*Transplant International*), is used by SATS to define each donation stage and is reproduced with SA-specific DBD/DCD branches in the *National Strategic Roadmap* (Sept 2024), included in the course manual pp.94–95 (internal PDF numbering).
- DBD (donation after brain death): family/legal consent → somatic support continues → transplant team takes over donor management (legally, only once consented, per National Health Act 61/2003) → organ retrieval.
- DCD (donation after circulatory death): occurs after an *independent* decision to withdraw non-beneficial treatment; two doctors (one independent of the transplant team, ≥5 years' registration) certify circulatory death; retrieval must begin rapidly given warm ischaemic time; a time-limited "stand-down" period applies if the patient doesn't arrest within an agreed window. — Western Cape policy circular H84/2025 (course manual, pp.7–10) and course manual's citation of a 2024 South African Journal of Bioethics and Law (SAJBL) article specifically addressing DCD's legality under Chapter 8 (see §2 and §6 — full text could not be retrieved for this pass).

## 2. Legal framework: National Health Act 61 of 2003, Regulations, and 2026 currency

- **National Health Act 61 of 2003, Chapter 8** (ss. covering donation of human bodies, tissue, blood, blood products, gametes; removal of tissue at post-mortem; consent; allocation/use; revocation; confidentiality) is the founding statute. It remains in force and is the framework all corpus sources (Document H, course manual, Western Cape circular) explicitly cite.
- **Regulations Regarding the General Control of Human Bodies, Tissue, Blood Products and Gametes** (made under Chapter 8; Government Notice R180, *Government Gazette* 35099, 2 March 2012; came into effect 1 March 2012) supply the operational detail: who may consent, certification-of-death requirements (two doctors, one ≥5 years' HPCSA registration, neither part of the transplant team), rules on payment (prohibited, but reasonable-cost reimbursement and professional fees are permitted), organ transplantation into non-SA-citizens/non-permanent-residents (requires Ministerial written authorisation), and revocation/conflicting-donation rules. — Primary secondary-legal-explainer source in the corpus: McQuoid-Mason, *S Afr Med J* 2012;102(9):733-735 (reproduced inside the course manual as a "supporting text").
- **Confirmed still current / actively maintained, as of this research (Sept 2026):**
  - Regulations under **GN 1434 of 2017** amended Tables 1–4 of the Regulations, per **Notice 6055, Government Gazette 52388, 28 February 2025** (source: acts.co.za consolidated National Health Act tracker). Exact table content (likely donor/consent-form schedules or fee/administration tables) was **not independently verified against the Gazette text itself** in this pass — flagged as a gap (§6).
  - A **Ministerial Advisory Committee on Organ Transplantation** was established by **Notice 5360, Government Gazette 51352, 4 October 2024** — a new institutional/governance development under the National Health Act, worth a course mention as a live 2024–2026 development, though no further detail on its mandate/output was found (gap, §6).
  - A **National Forensic Pathology Services Committee Terms of Reference** notice (Notice 5862, GG 52107, 14 February 2025) is relevant to the forensic-pathologist-authorisation step required in unnatural/medico-legal death cases.
- **No evidence of a shift toward presumed consent / opt-out.** South Africa remains an explicit-consent ("opt-in") jurisdiction. A September 2025 secondary source (*Spotlight*) surveys the research literature and reports expert consensus that switching South Africa to an opt-out default, absent parallel investment in healthcare capacity, public awareness, and family-counselling infrastructure, "is unlikely to increase organ donations" — i.e., current policy direction favours strengthening the opt-in system (palliative-care standards, staff training, institutional protocols, outcome audits) rather than legislative reform of the consent default. This is corroborated by academic literature (e.g., a Wits University thesis exploring opt-in/opt-out/mandated-choice models) that treats opt-in as the status quo baseline for South Africa.

## 3. Ethical topics: HPCSA guidelines and consent-model status

- **HPCSA Booklet 4 – "Seeking Patients' Informed Consent: The Ethical Considerations"** (revised December 2021, still current) supplies the general medical-ethics doctrine underpinning donation consent: patient/family autonomy, sufficiency of information, voluntariness, capacity assessment, third-party/surrogate consent, and the statutory link to the National Health Act and the Constitution. Read directly (course manual excerpt, pp.1+). This is a **general** informed-consent guideline, not donation-specific.
- **HPCSA Booklet 7 – "Guidelines for the Withholding and Withdrawing of Treatment"** was **revised in September 2025 and publicly announced 20 November 2025** — this is *more current* than the "HPCSA Guidelines on Palliative Care – Booklet 17" cited as a "supporting text" in the course manual's table of contents. Booklet 7 directly addresses end-of-life decision-making (best-interests principle, futility, patient representatives, documentation requirements) that underlies the ethics of withdrawing treatment prior to DCD. **Recommendation:** source the current Booklet 7 directly for Intermediate Level ethics content on withdrawal-of-treatment/DCD, rather than relying only on the older Booklet 17 reference in the course manual.
- **Consent model status (repeated from §2 for the ethics framing):** South Africa uses an explicit, opt-in, next-of-kin consent model; ODF registration records intent only. The live ethical debate in the literature is autonomy-of-the-deceased vs. family-veto-in-practice (families can and sometimes do decline even when the deceased was a registered donor) — this tension is explicitly named in academic sources (e.g. the SciELO article "Autonomy in organ donations v family consent: A South African legislative context") and is a natural Intermediate-Level ethics discussion point, though that specific article is a secondary/academic source found via web search, not part of the Source Corpus.
- Family-facing ethical practice (dignity, decoupling, non-coercion, continued support regardless of decision, second medical opinion offered to families who want it) is documented in the CCSSA guideline (§1.2) and the FACTS protocol (§1.3) — both are appropriate primary anchors for "ethics of the conversation," separate from the higher-level "ethics of the consent model" discussion above.
- No standalone, current HPCSA guideline exists specifically titled/dedicated to organ or tissue donation ethics; donation ethics must be synthesised from the general Informed Consent and Withholding/Withdrawing Treatment booklets, applied to the donation context — this synthesis point itself is worth stating explicitly in the course content so learners understand why the ethics section draws on general medical-ethics guidelines rather than a donation-specific HPCSA text.

## 5. Currency notes — is each source still authoritative, or superseded?

1. **CCSSA determination-of-death guideline**: the corpus draft PDF is superseded *within the corpus itself* by the published, peer-reviewed `SAJCC-37-1-466.pdf` (2021). That published version remains the current SA standard as of this research. However, the guideline's own text sets a **5-year review cycle**, meaning a review was due in **2026 (this year)**; no confirmed revised/replacement guideline was found via web search as of this research pass. **Action for course build:** re-check for a published update closer to launch.
2. **Excellence in Deceased Donation Course Manual (Updated 2025)**: this document is **not a purely South African primary source**. Large sections (the "Elements 1–5" family-donation-conversation framework, "DonateLife," "Australian Organ Donor Register (AODR)," ANZICS references, Appendix A–D templates) are drawn from the **Australian Organ & Tissue Authority's "Best Practice Guideline for Offering Organ and Tissue Donation in Australia."** South African specificity comes from appended/inserted material: the Western Cape provincial circular (H84/2025, dated 19 June 2025 — the single most current dated primary document in the whole corpus), the SATS *National Strategic Roadmap* (Sept 2024), the 2012 SAMJ legal explainer, and HPCSA booklet excerpts. **Course-build implication:** content drafters must not present Australian bodies/terminology (DonateLife, AODR) as South African; the generic international best-practice process content should be clearly separated from SA-specific legal/regulatory fact content when writing lessons.
3. **National Health Act 61/2003 and its Regulations**: still the current governing law; actively maintained, with regulation-table amendments as recently as **28 February 2025** and a new Ministerial Advisory Committee on Organ Transplantation established **4 October 2024**. Confirmed current as of Sept 2026 research, subject to the gap noted in §6 about not having pulled the Gazette text directly.
4. **HPCSA Booklet 4 (Informed Consent)**: current (revised Dec 2021, no newer version found).
5. **HPCSA Booklet 17 (Palliative Care)**, as cited in the course manual: likely superseded in relevance (not necessarily withdrawn) by the more directly on-point **HPCSA Booklet 7 (Withholding/Withdrawing Treatment), revised September 2025** — a materially more current and more topically relevant HPCSA source for DCD/end-of-life ethics content than what the course manual itself cites.
6. **Consent model (opt-in)**: stable and current — no legislative movement toward presumed consent found; recent (Sept 2025) expert commentary explicitly argues against an opt-out shift for South Africa absent other investment.
7. **SAJBL 2024 DCD-legality article**: referenced by the course manual as a supporting text but its full content could not be retrieved in this research pass (403 error on one URL, binary/unparseable PDF on another). Its currency/content should be verified directly before the Intermediate Level relies on it for DCD-legality claims.

## 6. Flagged gaps

- **Allocation/matching criteria**: no document in the Source Corpus describes how organs are actually allocated to waitlisted patients (matching criteria, waiting-time weighting, geographic/priority rules). Only passing references to a "national priority listing" and "extended criteria donors" exist. This sub-topic cannot be fully grounded from the current corpus alone.
- **Primary legislative text**: the actual Government Gazette text of NHA Chapter 8 and the Regulations Regarding General Control of Human Bodies, Tissue, Blood Products and Gametes (as amended 2017 and Feb 2025) is not in the corpus — only a 2012 secondary/explainer article (McQuoid-Mason, SAMJ) is present. Recommend pulling the Gazette PDF(s) directly for exact-language citation if the Curriculum Spec wants to quote the law rather than paraphrase a 13-year-old secondary summary of it.
- **SAJBL 2024 DCD-legality article**: full text not retrieved (see §5, point 7) — needs direct sourcing before the build team relies on its specific legal conclusions.
- **Ministerial Advisory Committee on Organ Transplantation** (est. Oct 2024): only its existence/gazette notice was found; no primary source describing its mandate, membership, or any output/recommendations. Worth flagging in course content as "a governance body was recently established" without over-claiming what it has done.
- **Donation-specific HPCSA guidance**: does not exist as a standalone booklet; ethics content must synthesise general Informed Consent (Booklet 4) and Withholding/Withdrawing Treatment (Booklet 7, 2025) guidance applied to the donation context — worth stating this explicitly for transparency rather than implying a dedicated HPCSA donation-ethics document exists.
- **Language/translation of consent conversations**: out of scope per `map.md` ("Not yet specified"), but the FACTS protocol explicitly discusses translator selection and involvement for family consent conversations across South Africa's 11 official languages — this is a live accessibility consideration for Intermediate Level content even though the broader translation-strategy question is deliberately deferred elsewhere in this project.
- **CCSSA guideline's due-for-review status**: flagged in §5, point 1 — re-check before Curriculum Spec finalisation.

## 7. Candidate topic list — grouped into Stages (3–5 expected; not forced)

**Stage 1 — How Donation Happens: The Process**
- Referral: who refers, when, and why timing matters
- Determining death: brain death and circulatory death, in plain language (grounded in the CCSSA/SAJCC guideline)
- DBD vs DCD explained
- Tissue donation vs organ donation — how the pathways differ

**Stage 2 — Consent: Whose Decision, and How**
- South Africa's opt-in consent model and what ODF registration actually does (and doesn't) guarantee
- The legal consent hierarchy (next-of-kin order of preference) under Chapter 8 of the National Health Act
- The family conversation: decoupling, dignity, non-coercion (FACTS protocol)
- HPCSA's general informed-consent principles applied to donation

**Stage 3 — The South African Legal Framework**
- National Health Act 61/2003, Chapter 8, and its Regulations — what the law actually requires
- Certification-of-death legal requirements (two doctors, independence from transplant team, HPCSA registration)
- No cost to the donor's family/estate; prohibition on organ trade
- Recent developments: 2024–2025 regulatory and governance updates (Ministerial Advisory Committee; regulation amendments)

**Stage 4 — Ethics of Donation and End-of-Life Care**
- Withdrawal-of-treatment ethics as it relates to DCD (HPCSA Booklet 7, 2025)
- Family accommodation, second opinions, and dignity in dying
- The opt-in vs. presumed-consent debate — why South Africa hasn't moved to opt-out, and what the evidence actually says would help
- Equity and scarcity: waitlist vs. transplant-rate reality, extended-criteria donors

**Stage 5 (optional) — Who's Who in the South African System**
- Organ Donor Foundation (awareness/registry, not medical/allocation)
- SATS / SATCS (professional and clinical coordination bodies)
- Tissue banks (Vitanova, Centre for Tissue Engineering) and transplant centres
- Useful for healthcare-adjacent learners; could be folded into Stage 1 instead if a 4-Stage structure is preferred.
