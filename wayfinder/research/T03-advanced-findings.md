## 1. Advocacy-oriented topics, with source citations

These are the topics that fit CONTEXT.md's definition of Advanced Level: "advocacy and greater depth for healthcare-adjacent learners." All four are directly supported by primary material already in the Source Corpus — no invented content was needed.

### 1.1 The family donation conversation (structured frameworks)

The Source Corpus contains **two independent, convergent frameworks** for having the donation conversation with a deceased patient's family — one South African, one Australian (which the SA Western Cape policy explicitly imports as its Appendix 3). Both agree on the same core sequence, which is strong grounds for treating it as the spine of an Advanced-Level Stage:

- **Separate the death conversation from the donation conversation.** Family must understand and accept the death (brain death or expected circulatory death) *before* donation is raised.
  — *Excellence in Deceased Donation Course Manual* ("Best Practice Guideline for Offering Organ and Tissue Donation in Australia", Element 2, p.68 of the compiled PDF / p.8 of the original guideline); *SATS Red File* §8.1, Wits Transplant FACTS Steps 2–4 (pp.10, 14, 16 of the FACTS excerpt); *7 Lives in 7 Steps* flowchart ("Inform family of diagnosis... DO NOT DISCUSS ORGAN DONATION").
- **Plan before you talk to the family** — a multidisciplinary team huddle (treating doctor, transplant coordinator, nurse, social worker/chaplain/interpreter as needed) agrees roles, wording, and timing before the family meeting.
  — Course Manual, Element 3 "Planning the approach" (p.69, orig. p.9) and Appendix C "FDC planning template" (p.77, orig. p.17); *Excellence...* Western Cape policy §6 "Approaching the family" (p.11); *SATS Red File* FACTS Step 1 "Planning" (p.12 of excerpt).
- **Collaborative delivery** — the treating doctor and the transplant coordinator/specialist nurse offer donation together; the coordinator is the one with donation-specific expertise and stays with the family through the decision and afterwards.
  — Course Manual, Element 4 "Discussing donation" (p.70, orig. p.10).
- **Debrief/review after every conversation**, feeding a continuous-improvement loop.
  — Course Manual, Element 5 "Reviewing practice" (p.71, orig. p.11) and Appendix D "FDC review template" (p.78–81, orig. p.19–21); Western Cape policy §17 "Quality improvement, reporting, and policy oversight" (p.17).
- **South Africa's own version of this framework**: the *SATS Red File* (§8.1, pp.10–24 of the excerpt) reproduces the **Wits Transplant "FACTS"** (Family Approach to Consent for Transplant Strategy) — an 8-step, SA-adapted version of the UK NHSBT approach, with a printable "Key Do's and Don'ts" table, two worked troubleshooting scenarios (a family that thinks it must decide to "switch off the machine"; a family in internal disagreement — explicitly framed as common in South Africa's larger extended-family structures), and a description of the **"donor pause"** — a locally-practised moment of silence/prayer in ICU and again in theatre, including a sample reading. This is South-African-specific, practitioner-tested, directly quotable content — probably the single best piece of source material for this Stage.

### 1.2 The transplant/donation coordinator's role

- **Definition and scope**: "TC – Transplant coordinator (who is a SNOD – Specialist nurse in organ donation)."
  — *Excellence...* Western Cape policy, Definitions §1 (p.8).
- **What the coordinator actually does**: screens referrals for donation suitability, is contacted *before* the family is told about end-of-life (so information like national-priority-list / Organ Donor Foundation registration status is ready), leads/collaborates on the family conversation, obtains forensic-pathology and medical-manager authorisation, coordinates theatre logistics, arranges family viewing after retrieval, sends "thank you" letters and provides bereavement support, and participates in audit/morbidity-and-mortality review of missed donation opportunities.
  — Western Cape policy §§2, 5–9, 13, 17, 19 (pp.8–19).
- **Professional body**: the **South African Transplant Coordinators Society (SATCS)**, founded 30 June 2017 as a special-interest group of SATS, exists "to educate, develop and support all transplant coordinators in South Africa" under a Code of Conduct, and produced the Red File itself as a hospital reference tool.
  — *SATS Red File*, "Who we are" (p.2 of the document).
- **Training note (external, not in corpus)**: SATS trains "transplant link nurses" and ICU specialists to improve identification/referral (per sats.org.za, confirmed via web search but not itself a corpus document — see gaps, §4).

### 1.3 Public-advocacy / awareness-raising skills

- **Media and family-privacy protocol**: hospitals/provinces run donation-awareness campaigns via broadcast/print media and the national donor register, but donor-family details are never publicised without the family's own wish, and a "cooling off" period is observed before any PR engagement.
  — Western Cape policy §§19, 21 "Media" (pp.14–15, note: the policy repeats this section twice under numbers 19 and 21 — a duplication in the source document itself, not a research error).
- **Equity/cultural-sensitivity in offering donation**: the policy is explicit that no community should be excluded from being asked, that assumptions about a family's ethnic/cultural/spiritual background must never be used to skip the conversation, and that a faith representative or hospital chaplain can be brought in to support the discussion.
  — Western Cape policy §18 "Equality and diversity" (pp.14–15).
- **South Africa-specific grassroots advocacy model**: the **Organ Donor Foundation of South Africa (ODF)**'s current flagship initiative is the **ULUNTU Awareness Campaign** ("humanity"/"community", isiXhosa) — a grassroots, community-participative programme explicitly targeting *cultural barriers to consent* in vulnerable communities, run in partnership with hospital transplant coordinators. ODF's stated dual mission is public awareness *and* educating medical professionals to consider donation when assessing brain-dead patients and to improve referral. (odf.org.za/projects/, odf.org.za/who-we-are/ — not in the Source Corpus; see gaps.)
- **SATS's own public-facing advocacy**: SATS members have run radio sessions and SABC 2 appearances to dispel donation myths (sats.org.za — external, not in corpus).

### 1.4 Consent and end-of-life ethics, at "greater depth"

This is where the Advanced Level's "greater depth for healthcare-adjacent learners" mandate is most literally satisfied by the corpus:

- **HPCSA Booklet 4, "Seeking Patients' Informed Consent: The Ethical Considerations"** (revised Dec 2021) — the full general medical-ethics framework for informed consent (what information must be given, who may obtain consent, capacity assessment, third-party/surrogate decision-making, emergencies) that underlies — but is broader than — the donation-specific next-of-kin consent conversation.
  — Course Manual, pp.86–102.
- **HPCSA Booklet 1/17, "Ethical Guidelines on Palliative Care"** (2019) — patient autonomy and advance directives/surrogate decision-making when a patient is incapacitated, the "best interests" test, and the four-principles framing (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) applied to end-of-life care, including a direct statement that palliative treatment that incidentally shortens life is not unlawful provided informed consent was given. (Note: the Course Manual's own table of contents labels this "Booklet 17"; the document's cover page prints "Booklet 1, 2019" — an inconsistency in the source material itself, not introduced by this research.)
  — Course Manual, pp.104–119.
- **Legal framework underneath the consent conversation** (Chapter 8 of the National Health Act 61 of 2003 and its regulations): order of preference among relatives/partners for consent, the two-independent-doctor death-certification requirement, the prohibition on payment for organs, and the Minister's-authorisation rule for transplanting into non-citizens.
  — Course Manual, SAMJ "Medicine and the Law" article by D McQuoid-Mason, *S Afr Med J* 2012;102(9):733–735, DOI:10.7196/SAMJ.6047 (pp.84–85, 103). This overlaps with Intermediate Level's "South African legal framework" remit (CONTEXT.md) — recommend Advanced Level engage it at the *application* level (how the law shapes the actual consent conversation) rather than re-teaching it from scratch.

### 1.5 What actually predicts a family's decision (the evidence base for the above)

- **anntransplant-22-17.pdf** (Han et al., *Ann Transplant* 2017;22:17–23) is itself a South-Korean single-center study of exactly this: family decision-*timing* and its correlation with consent. See full verdict in §2 below — it is graded as substantially direct-content material, not background.
- Referenced secondary evidence inside the Australian FDC guideline (Course Manual pp.64–65, orig. pp.4–5): consent rates are measurably higher when a trained Donation Specialist Nurse is involved in the conversation (54% vs 33% trained-non-specialist vs 28% untrained staff, citing Radford et al. 2020), and both family satisfaction and consent rates are sensitive to perceived quality of care, information adequacy, and staff training (Simpkin et al. 2009 *BMJ*; Siminoff et al. 2001 *JAMA* — both cited, not themselves in the corpus).

## 2. Verdicts on the four clinical/surgical papers

The ticket asked me not to assume the answer and to actually check each paper's abstract/conclusions. Doing so changed the picture for one of the four.

| Source | Verdict | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Liver Transplantation 2009 — Porrett, "Late Surgical Complications Following Liver Transplantation"** | **Background/citation only.** No direct-content extraction. | Pure post-transplant surgical morbidity review for clinicians managing transplant recipients years later: biliary strictures, hepatic artery thrombosis/stenosis, portal vein thrombosis, incisional hernias, by donor type (deceased/living) and recipient age. Abstract's "Key Points" are entirely procedural/surgical (endoscopic vs surgical management of strictures, retransplantation indications). Nothing about donation, family conversations, or the coordinator role. Squarely inside the map's "deep surgical/clinical detail is background, not direct course content" carve-out. |
| **Mancini & Lietz 2010 — "Selection of Cardiac Transplantation Candidates in 2010"** (Circulation) | **Background/citation only.** No direct-content extraction. | Recipient-side selection criteria for heart transplant candidacy: absolute/relative contraindications (malignancy history, AIDS, fixed pulmonary hypertension thresholds, age, active infection), UNOS listing-status mechanics, mechanical circulatory support as bridge-to-transplant. This is transplant-physician decision-support content about *who gets listed to receive an organ*, not about donation, advocacy, or the coordinator/family-conversation skillset. Its Conclusions section is entirely about the evolving landscape of LVAD-as-destination-therapy and candidate selection trends. |
| **PIIS1053249814011814.pdf — ISHLT Consensus, "Selection of Lung Transplant Candidates: 2014 update"** | **Background/citation only.** No direct-content extraction. | Same genre as the Mancini/Lietz paper: an international consensus document on referral timing and disease-specific selection criteria (COPD, cystic fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, interstitial lung disease) for the *recipient* side of lung transplantation. Its own abstract frames the goal as helping physicians "properly identify patients who are most likely to benefit" — again recipient candidacy, not donation/advocacy. One line ("early referral allows... thorough patient education") gestures at communication but is about patient education for transplant candidates, not next-of-kin donation conversations, and is too thin to build content on. |
| **anntransplant-22-17.pdf — Han et al. 2017, "Factors Associated with a Family's Delay of Decision for Organ Donation After Brain Death"** | **Substantially direct content — this one does NOT belong in the "background only" bucket the ticket grouped it into.** | This is not a surgical/clinical-management paper at all: it is a retrospective study of *family decision-making behaviour* around donation consent. Its subject matter is squarely advocacy-adjacent: it describes the OPO (Organ Procurement Organisation) coordinator's counseling workflow, quantifies that ~14% of families take >48 hours to decide and that this delay does *not* reduce consent or successful-donation rates, identifies clinical predictors of a slower decision (hypotension, coma therapy), discusses cultural factors specific to Confucian-tradition societies affecting willingness to donate, and its explicit practical conclusion is: "the medical attendant and OPO coordinator should continue the efforts to maintain organ viability and consider extended, repetitive counseling to encourage donation" rather than treating a slow decision as a lost cause. **Direct-content use**: the finding that decision delay ≠ lower consent, and the recommendation for patient, repeated, respectful counseling, support the "give families time, don't rush or write off a slow decision" teaching point for the family-conversation Stage — with the caveat that it is a single-center Korean study (n=107) and its cultural-context claims should be presented as illustrative rather than generalised to South Africa. The clinical predictor variables (MAP, serum sodium, coma-therapy status) and the Methods/Results tables remain background/citation only. |

**Net effect on the ticket's framing**: three of the four clinical papers (Porrett, Mancini/Lietz, ISHLT lung) confirm the map's Note that "deep surgical/clinical detail is background, not direct course content" — they are legitimately citation-only, useful for footnoting "why is the recipient list so selective / why do organs remain scarce" if that context is ever wanted, but nothing more. The fourth (Han et al.) was mis-filed by the ticket's premise: it is a family-decision-making study, not a surgical-outcomes study, and its core finding is directly usable Advanced Level content.

## 4. Flagged gaps

Material this Level plausibly needs that the Source Corpus does **not** cover, based on the above reading plus a light external check (web search/fetch of odf.org.za, sats.org.za, and one PMC article — done only to *characterise* the gap, not to smuggle it in as course content):

1. **No SA-specific data on healthcare-worker donation-conversation training gaps.** Green et al. 2023 (*Nursing Reports* 13(1):255–264, Eastern Cape) found only 62.7% of critical-care nurses surveyed had adequate organ-donation knowledge, 53% positive attitudes, and 49.7% good practice-readiness, attributing this to "limited or non-existing organ donation modules in nursing training curricula" and a lack of clarity in nurses' formal scope of practice around donation. This is exactly the audience gap Advanced Level exists to close, but it's an external citation, not a corpus source — worth citing in the curriculum rationale, not as course content per se.
2. **The full Wits Transplant Procurement Handbook is not in the corpus** — only a ~15-page excerpt (the FACTS section) is reprinted inside the SATS Red File. The complete handbook is free at `dgmc.co.za/docs/Wits-Transplant-Procurement-Handbook.pdf` per the Red File's own citation, and may contain more (e.g. deeper troubleshooting scenarios, tissue-donation-specific consent scripts) worth pulling in if this Stage goes deep.
3. **The NHSBT "Approaching the Families of Potential Organ Donors: Best Practice Guidance" (2013)** is the common ancestor of both the Australian FDC framework and the Wits FACTS strategy, and is directly cited by both, but is not itself in the corpus (only referenced/adapted). Low priority since both derivatives are already present and SA-adapted, but the original could resolve any remaining ambiguity if content authors want the "source of the source."
4. **No current (2026) ODF or SATS *public-facing* advocacy campaign materials are in the corpus.** ODF's ULUNTU Awareness Campaign (cultural-barrier-focused, community-participative, grassroots) is exactly the kind of "public advocacy skills" model CONTEXT.md envisions for this Level, but only a general description is available from odf.org.za — no campaign toolkit, training curriculum, or volunteer materials were found in a light web pass. If "public advocacy skills" becomes its own Stage (rather than folded into the family-conversation Stage), reaching out to ODF/SATS directly for volunteer/advocate training materials would likely be worthwhile before drafting lesson content.
5. **No corpus material on bereavement support / donor-family aftercare** beyond brief mentions (thank-you letters, "donor pause," media-privacy protocol). If the course wants a dedicated bereavement-support topic (distinct from the consent conversation itself), this would need new sourcing — HPCSA's Palliative Care booklet touches bereavement only tangentially (it's about the dying patient, not the donor family afterward).
6. **No SATCS/SATS formal transplant-coordinator training-curriculum document** (only its existence, purpose and Code of Conduct are described). If "what a coordinator's actual training and qualifications look like" needs to go deeper than role description, this would need to come from SATS/SATCS directly.
7. **The video, `The_Journey_of_a_Gift.mp4`, was not screened** in this research pass (out of scope for a document read). It should be screened before Stage content is finalized — donor-family-story videos are a natural fit for the family-conversation Stage but need a content check first (tone, whether it centers a South African story, whether it's usable as-is or needs re-licensing).

## 5. Candidate topic list (grouped into Stages)

Four Stages emerge cleanly from the material above, each backed by direct-content sources; none are forced.

**Stage A — The transplant/donation coordinator's role**
Definition (TC = SNOD); when and why they're brought in (before the family is told about end-of-life, not after); what they do end-to-end (screening → planning → the conversation → authorisation/logistics → aftercare → audit); SATCS as the professional body.
*Sources:* Course Manual Western Cape policy §§1–2, 5, 8–9, 13, 17 (pp.8–19); SATS Red File "Who we are" (p.2).

**Stage B — Having the donation conversation**
The 5-element / 8-step frameworks (planning → separating death from donation → collaborative delivery → debrief); the SA-specific Wits FACTS script with Do's/Don'ts; the two SA-flavoured troubleshooting scenarios (machine-switch-off misconception; family disagreement); the "donor pause."
*Sources:* Course Manual "Best Practice Guideline for Offering Organ and Tissue Donation" + FDC templates (pp.61–81); SATS Red File §8.1 (Wits FACTS, pp.10–24 of excerpt); 7 Lives in 7 Steps flowchart; anntransplant-22-17.pdf (decision-timing finding, with caveats).

**Stage C — Consent and end-of-life ethics, in depth**
HPCSA informed-consent framework applied to next-of-kin donation consent; capacity, surrogates, and advance directives from the Palliative Care booklet; the National Health Act Chapter 8 rules that shape who may consent and how (built on, not duplicating, Intermediate Level's legal-framework coverage).
*Sources:* Course Manual HPCSA Booklet 4 (pp.86–102); HPCSA Palliative Care booklet (pp.104–119); SAMJ legal article (pp.84–85, 103).

**Stage D — Public advocacy: equity, media, and community trust**
Equity/cultural-sensitivity obligations in who gets asked; donor-family privacy and media protocol; South Africa's own advocacy model (ODF's ULUNTU campaign, cultural-barrier-focused grassroots outreach) as a case study in *why* culturally-attuned advocacy matters, distinct from Stage B's one-to-one conversation skills.
*Sources:* Course Manual Western Cape policy §§18–19, 21 (pp.14–15); ODF materials (external — see gap #4, would need direct outreach for anything beyond framing).

A fifth Stage was considered and rejected: splitting "the coordinator's role" (Stage A) from "having the conversation" (Stage B) risks being too granular given how tightly the corpus interleaves them (the same Western Cape policy sections and the same FDC framework cover both); they're kept separate above only because CONTEXT.md's Stage count (3–5) comfortably allows it and the two have genuinely distinct learning objectives (a role/systems Stage vs. a communication-skills Stage). If the finalize ticket (T06) wants to compress to 3 Stages, merging A+B is the natural fold; Stage C and D stand on their own regardless.
