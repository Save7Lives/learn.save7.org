## The consent model

South Africa uses an explicit-consent, **opt-in** model. There is no presumed consent. Stage 4 covers the debate about changing it.

## Section 62 of the National Health Act 61 of 2003

**Section 62(1): the person's own donation.** A person competent to make a will may donate their body or any specified tissue, for use after death:

- in a will;
- in a document signed by them and at least two competent witnesses; or
- in an oral statement made in the presence of at least two competent witnesses.

Section 65 allows a donation to be revoked in the same way it was made, or by intentionally destroying the will or document.

**Section 62(2): the family's donation.** In the absence of a donation under section 62(1), or of a contrary direction given by the person while alive, the following may donate after the person's death, in the specific order mentioned: spouse, partner, major child, parent, guardian, major brother, major sister.

**Section 62(3).** If none of those people can be located, the Director-General may donate specific tissue, but only once all the prescribed steps to locate them have been taken.

**Not to be confused with section 7.** Consent to *treatment* for a living patient who cannot consent follows a different order: spouse or partner, then parent, grandparent, adult child, brother or sister.

## The ODF register

- Registration is free and quick, and records a person's intention to donate.
- SATCS Red File: registering "does not mean that the donor's organs will automatically be donated at the time of death"; consent from the next of kin is always required.
- SATS national strategy roadmap: the register represents an expression of intent, and families are approached for consent in every case where there is a potential donor.
- The 2012 regulations say a doctor *may* act on a will or document that donates tissue, if it appears valid on its face. They do not say a doctor must.

**The law-practice gap.** In law, relatives' power to donate arises only in the absence of the person's own donation. In practice, families are approached in every case and a refusal is respected. Slabbert and Venter (*De Jure*, 2019) examine this gap as a weakness in protecting donor autonomy.

**The advocacy line:** register, and then tell your family.

## The family conversation

- **Decoupling.** The treating doctor informs the family of the death. Donation is raised later, usually by the transplant coordinator, once the death is understood and accepted.
- **FACTS** (Family Approach to Consent for Transplant Strategy), Wits Transplant. 📌 An adaptation of the NHS Blood and Transplant strategy for approaching the families of potential organ donors, modified for South African hospitals. Eight steps: planning; breaking bad news; a time-out; assessing understanding and acceptance of the loss; the consent conversation; a second time-out; a final family discussion; and follow-up, feedback and support.
- **Non-coercion.** No pressure, no rushing, and a decision that is accepted whatever it is.

## HPCSA Booklet 4, applied

*Seeking Patients' Informed Consent: The Ethical Considerations*, revised December 2021. It does not mention donation; its principles are applied here by extension.

- Information sufficient to the decision, in a language the person understands.
- Arrangements for language needs, including independent interpreters.
- Practitioners must check how well consent was understood, not rely on the form in which it was recorded.
- Practitioners may recommend but must not pressure, and must declare conflicts of interest.

## Recording consent in practice

The Red File records that consent for deceased organ and tissue donation is obtained from the next of kin in writing or by telephone, with two witnesses.
