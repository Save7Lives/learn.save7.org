- **South Africa is opt-in.** Nobody is presumed to be a donor. Donation needs a positive decision, by the person while alive or by their family after death.

- **The person comes first.** Section 62 of the National Health Act lets anyone competent to make a will donate in a will, in a document signed with two competent witnesses, or in an oral statement before two competent witnesses.

- **The family decides only in the absence of that, and in a fixed order:** spouse, partner, major child, parent, guardian, major brother, major sister. A partner is included, "major" means adult, and it is a priority order, not a committee.

- **A "no" given while alive is protected too.** A contrary direction removes the family's power to donate.

- **Registering records your wish; it does not decide on its own.** The Red File says registration does not mean organs will automatically be donated, and in practice the family is approached in every case and a refusal is respected.

- **Say both halves.** "Your family can't override you" is wrong about practice. "Registering is pointless" is wrong about the law. The accurate line is: register, and then tell your family.

- **The death and the donation are separate conversations.** Decoupling means the family understands and accepts the death before donation is raised, usually by the coordinator.

- **FACTS is how South African coordinators approach families.** 📌 It is Wits Transplant's adaptation of UK NHS Blood and Transplant guidance: planned, with interpreters, time alone for the family, and a decision that is accepted whatever it is.

- **HPCSA has no donation-specific guideline.** Its general informed-consent principles (Booklet 4) apply by extension: enough information, in a language understood, understanding checked, and no pressure.
