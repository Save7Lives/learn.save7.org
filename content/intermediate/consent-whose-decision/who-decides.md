## What the law says

Consent to donation after death is governed by **section 62 of the National Health Act 61 of 2003**. It works in two layers, and the order matters.

**First, the person themselves.** Anyone competent to make a will may donate their body, or specified tissue, to be used after their death, in one of three ways:

- in their **will**;
- in a **document** signed by them and by at least two competent witnesses; or
- in an **oral statement** made in the presence of at least two competent witnesses.

A donation made this way can be revoked the same way it was made, or, if it was in a will or document, by deliberately destroying that document.

**Second, the family, but only in the absence of the first.** Where the person made no such donation, and gave no contrary direction while alive, the Act allows these people to donate, **in the specific order mentioned**:

1. spouse
2. partner
3. major child
4. parent
5. guardian
6. major brother
7. major sister

Three details are routinely got wrong:

- **A partner is included**, and ranks above every relative except a spouse.
- **"Major" means adult.** A child or sibling under 18 cannot consent.
- **It is an order, not a committee.** The Act does not hand the decision to "the family" as a group. It sets a priority.

One more detail matters just as much. A person who said **no** while alive has given a "contrary direction", and the family's power to donate falls away. A clearly stated refusal is protected by the law too.

And if none of the listed people can be found, the Act lets the Director-General of Health donate specific tissue, but only after every prescribed step to locate them has been taken.

**Don't confuse this list with a different one.** The same Act has a separate order for who may consent to *treatment* on behalf of a living patient who cannot decide: spouse or partner, then parent, grandparent, adult child, brother or sister. That order applies while the patient is alive. After death, for donation, it is the section 62 order above.

## What registering does, and what it doesn't

Registering with the **Organ Donor Foundation (ODF)** takes a few minutes online, costs nothing, and puts your decision on record. It is worth doing.

But it does not decide the matter on its own, and the organisations closest to the process say so plainly:

- The SATCS Red File, the coordinators' reference, states that registering as a donor **does not mean that the donor's organs will automatically be donated** at the time of death, and that consent from the next of kin is always required.
- The Southern African Transplantation Society's national strategy roadmap describes the ODF register as **an expression of an individual's intent to donate**, and notes that families are approached for consent in every case where there is a potential donor.

So there are two halves, and both must be said together.

**In law**, a donation made in one of the ways section 62 describes belongs to the person, and relatives have power to donate only in its absence. South African legal scholars, notably Slabbert and Venter, have written about the gap between that and what happens in hospitals, and treat it as a real weakness in how a donor's own choice is protected.

**In practice**, hospitals approach the family in every case, and a family's refusal is respected.

Telling someone "your family can't override you" misdescribes what will actually happen. Telling them "registering is pointless" misdescribes the law, and throws away the most useful thing registration does: it tells your family what you wanted. When the family is asked, the real question is *"what would they have wanted?"* Only a family that has heard the answer can give it.

**Register, and then tell your family.** The register records the decision. The conversation is what your family will be asked to act on.

## The family conversation

Stage 1 showed where this conversation sits in the process. Here is how it is meant to happen, and why.

**It is separated from the news of death.** The treating doctor tells the family that their relative has died, in words that leave no doubt that brain death is death. Donation is raised only later, usually by the transplant coordinator, once the family has understood and accepted the death. This is **decoupling**. It means the death is never heard as a request, and a family is never asked to absorb a death and make a donation decision in the same breath.

**It is planned, and it is unhurried.** Transplant coordinators in South Africa use a structured approach called **FACTS**, the Family Approach to Consent for Transplant Strategy, developed by Wits Transplant. 📌 It is an adaptation of the UK's NHS Blood and Transplant strategy for approaching the families of potential donors, modified for South African hospitals. It is not taken from the Australian material that appears in some donation training manuals.

FACTS has eight steps. You do not need them by heart; Advanced Level works through them. What matters here is what they are built to protect:

- the approach is **planned** in advance: who will be there, where, and with an **interpreter** if one is needed;
- the family is given **time alone**, twice: once after the news of death, and again after donation is raised;
- someone checks what the family has actually **understood**, before donation is mentioned;
- the family's **decision is accepted**, and support continues afterwards, whatever they decided.

**Dignity and non-coercion.** Nobody should be pressured, rushed or made to feel that one answer is expected. A family that says no has not failed, and nobody has failed them. A family that felt respected while declining is a better outcome than one that felt worked on.

## Informed consent, applied to donation

The Health Professions Council of South Africa (HPCSA) has no guideline written specifically for organ donation. What it has is general guidance on informed consent, **Booklet 4**, *Seeking Patients' Informed Consent: The Ethical Considerations* (revised December 2021). It is written for consent to a patient's own treatment, so using it for a family's decision about a relative who has died is an application of its principles, not something the booklet itself says. The principles transfer well:

- **Enough information to decide.** Booklet 4 says the amount of information needed varies with what is at stake. For donation, a family needs to know what would be recovered, what happens to their relative's body, and that the funeral can still go ahead.
- **In a language they understand.** Booklet 4 expects practitioners to meet language needs, for example through independent interpreters. Consent that is not understood is not informed consent, which makes an interpreter a safeguard, not a courtesy.
- **Understanding, not just a signature.** Practitioners must check how well consent has been understood, and not simply rely on the form in which it was recorded.
- **Free of pressure.** Practitioners may recommend, but must not put pressure on anyone to accept their advice, and must declare any conflict of interest. In donation, the separation between the treating team and the transplant team is how that conflict is kept out of the room.

In practice, the Red File records that consent for deceased donation is obtained from the next of kin in writing or by telephone, with two witnesses.
