## Withdrawing treatment: HPCSA Booklet 7

*Guidelines for the Withholding and Withdrawing of Treatment*, revised September 2025. Not Booklet 17 (palliative care), which some training material cites. Booklet 7 does not mention donation; its principles are applied to it here.

- It is not a practitioner's duty to prolong life at all costs.
- Any intervention whose primary intention is to end the patient's life is contrary to the ethics of health care and unlawful.
- The decision should ideally be made by the senior practitioner responsible, after consulting colleagues and taking into account the views of the patient or those close to them.
- Where the patient cannot decide and there is no advance directive, close family must be consulted and the decision taken in the patient's best interests.
- Never act in haste. Withholding treatment does not remove the duty to relieve the dying person's suffering.
- Practitioners should consider a second opinion where necessary; patients and family members have the right to seek one.
- Families requesting treatment considered futile must be given the reasons and the option of transfer. Serious disagreement goes to an independent clinical or ethical review, then legal advice.
- Patients should be able to die with dignity, with their families involved. Cultural practices around death and dying should be respected where known.

## Withdrawal and DCD

Western Cape Government Health circular H84/2025: donation after circulatory death is considered only after an independent decision by the treating team to withdraw treatment, in line with palliative care. No action is taken to hasten the dying. If the patient does not die within a pre-agreed period, the retrieval team stands down and palliative care continues with the treating team.

## The family: SA determination-of-death guidelines

- **Accommodation:** a period of somatic support so the family can process the death. Reasonable for a brief, finite period, with the timeframe given in advance; ordinarily not more than 24 hours.
- It is ethically and legally appropriate to end support once the family has been counselled and donation explored.
- Support should be discontinued, even during accommodation, if the bed is needed for a living patient and no other is available.
- **Second opinion:** another clinician in the hospital can give the family a second opinion on the brain-death determination, if that may help them accept the death.

## Opt-in and opt-out

South Africa: explicit consent (opt-in). Opt-out: presumed or deemed consent unless an objection was recorded; "soft" opt-out still involves the family.

📌 Non-South African evidence:

- Rithalia et al., *BMJ* 2009 (systematic review): presumed consent alone is unlikely to explain the variation in donation rates between countries.
- Shepherd et al., *BMC Medicine* 2014 (48 countries): more deceased donors under opt-out, fewer living donors.
- Arshad et al., *Kidney International* 2019 (35 OECD countries): no significant difference in deceased donation or transplant rates; fewer living donors under opt-out.
- Wales (soft opt-out from December 2015): a modest rise in family consent after about three years (Madden et al., *Anaesthesia* 2020), but no step change in donation (Noyes et al., *BMJ Open* 2019). England (from 2020): consent rates not yet increased (McLaughlin et al., *Transplantation* 2025).
- Spain: attributes its high donation rate to organisation, with transplant coordination teams in every donor hospital, not to its opt-out law (Matesanz and Domínguez-Gil, *Kidney International* 2019).

South African sources:

- Etheredge, *Risk Management and Healthcare Policy* 2021: little difference between the systems when used in isolation; barriers must be addressed at several levels.
- SATS national strategy roadmap: in the context of sustained low consent rates and low public awareness, no evidence supports an assumption of majority presumed consent.
- de Jager et al., *SAMJ* 2019 (the Wits transplant procurement model): donor numbers rose by targeting referral and family consent.

## Scarcity and fairness

- **The gap (SATS/SATCS, 2021):** 2,586 on the waiting list at year end; 229 solid organ transplants; 189 deaths on the waiting list.
- **Access (SATS roadmap):** transplant services concentrated in large urban areas in wealthier provinces, and more available to people with private health care; public-sector consent rates in the Western Cape far below private-sector rates.
- **Who is asked:** Western Cape circular H84/2025. No community is excluded, and a family's ethnic, cultural or spiritual background must never be the reason donation is not raised.
- **Who receives:** Booklet 7: no prejudice on grounds such as age, disability, race, culture, beliefs, sexuality, gender, lifestyle or social or economic status. Draft Regulations on Organ Transplantation (September 2026, not in force): allocation may not consider race, religion, culture or similar aspects of the deceased donor's life.
- **Extended selection criteria (SATCS Red File):** donors with comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes and HIV may be considered, with each organ assessed individually; outcomes are reported as excellent, with a significant survival benefit over no transplant.
- **Financial neutrality (SATS roadmap):** donors and families should neither lose nor gain; paying funeral costs is not an ethical solution.
