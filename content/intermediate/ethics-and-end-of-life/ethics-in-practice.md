## Withdrawing treatment is its own decision

Donation after circulatory death begins with a decision to withdraw life-sustaining treatment. So the first ethical question is whether that decision is ever made *for* donation. The answer South African practice gives is an unconditional no, and it is worth understanding why.

The relevant guidance is the HPCSA's **Booklet 7**, *Guidelines for the Withholding and Withdrawing of Treatment*, revised in September 2025. Some donation training material cites Booklet 17, on palliative care, instead. Booklet 7 is the one that governs this decision. It does not mention donation at all, so what follows applies its principles to donation rather than quoting a rule about it.

Booklet 7 starts from a plain position: **it is not a health practitioner's duty to prolong life at all costs.** Treatment that can no longer benefit a patient may be withheld or withdrawn, allowing death to follow its natural course. The booklet also draws a hard line on the other side: any intervention whose primary intention is to end a patient's life is both unethical and unlawful. Withdrawing treatment that no longer helps is not the same as ending a life, and the guidance treats the two as opposites.

The decision belongs to the patient's own care:

- It should ideally be made by the **senior practitioner responsible for the patient**, after consulting colleagues and taking into account the views of the patient or those close to them.
- Where the patient cannot take part and has left no advance directive, **the family must be consulted**, and the decision taken in the patient's **best interests**.
- The team should discuss the decision carefully and **never act in haste**.
- Withdrawing treatment does not end the duty of care. The practitioner must still relieve the patient's suffering as they die.

**Now add donation.** The Western Cape's 2025 donation policy puts the order beyond doubt. Donation is considered only **after an independent decision by the treating team** to withdraw treatment, in line with palliative care. Nothing is done to hasten the dying. If the heart does not stop in time, donation stands down and palliative care simply continues.

Why insist on that order? Because the moment the prospect of donation influences whether, or when, treatment is withdrawn, the patient stops being cared for as a person and starts being managed as a source of organs. The treating team's duty is to this patient alone. Keeping the withdrawal decision first, separate, and made for the patient's own reasons is what protects that duty, and it is also what makes it honest to tell a family that donation had nothing to do with it.

## The family at the end of life

**Accommodation.** After brain death has been determined, a family may ask for more time before the ventilator is switched off. The South African guidelines on the determination of death call this **accommodation**: a period of continued support that allows the family to come to terms with the death. The guidelines treat it as reasonable, with limits. It should be brief and finite, the family should be told the timeframe in advance, and ordinarily it should not go beyond a day.

The guidelines are also candid about where accommodation ends. It is ethically and legally appropriate to stop support once the family has been properly counselled about the death and the option of donation has been explored. And even where a family has asked for more time, support should stop if the bed is needed for a living patient and no other bed is available. That is a hard sentence to read. It is also honest about scarcity, and an advocate who knows it will not promise a family something the hospital cannot give.

**Second opinions.** Families sometimes cannot accept a diagnosis of death, or disagree with a decision to withdraw treatment. Both sets of guidance make room for that:

- The determination-of-death guidelines allow **another clinician in the hospital to give a second opinion** on a brain-death determination, where that may help the family accept the death.
- Booklet 7 says practitioners should consider seeking a second opinion where necessary, and that **patients and families have the right to seek one**.
- Where a family asks for treatment the team considers futile, Booklet 7 says they must be given the reasons, and told they may move the patient to another institution that will provide it. Where serious disagreement remains, it should go to a **clinical or ethical review independent of the team**, and if that fails, to legal advice on whether a court should decide.

None of this is about winning an argument with a grieving family. It is about making sure that a decision this final is one they can trust.

**Dignity in dying.** Booklet 7's stated purpose includes making sure that **patients can die with dignity** and that their families are properly involved in their care. It asks for conversations that are calm, honest, respectful and compassionate, without medical jargon. It asks practitioners to respect cultural practices around death and dying where they are known, and to explain the circumstances of a death to the family afterwards. In DCD, the Western Cape policy allows treatment to be withdrawn where the family can be present, if they wish to be. Donation, when it happens, happens inside a good death. It never replaces one.

## Opt-in or opt-out?

South Africa is **opt-in**: donation needs explicit consent, from the person or their family. Many countries use **opt-out**, also called **presumed** or **deemed consent**: everyone is treated as willing to donate unless they recorded an objection. Most opt-out systems are "soft", which means the family is still consulted.

It is tempting to assume opt-out would solve South Africa's shortage. The evidence says it is not that simple. 📌 All of the following comes from outside South Africa:

- A **systematic review** in the *BMJ* (Rithalia and colleagues, 2009) concluded that presumed consent alone is unlikely to explain the differences in donation rates between countries. Legislation, the number of potential donors, the organisation and infrastructure of the transplant service, wealth and investment in health care, and public attitudes may all play a part.
- **Comparisons between countries disagree.** A 2014 study of 48 countries (Shepherd and colleagues) found more deceased donors in opt-out countries, but fewer living donors. A 2019 study of 35 OECD countries (Arshad and colleagues) found no significant difference in deceased donation or overall transplant rates, and again fewer living donors under opt-out.
- **Wales** moved to soft opt-out in December 2015. Early evaluation found a modest rise in family consent after about three years, but no step change in donation. A later evaluation of **England's** 2020 switch reported that consent rates in both countries had not yet risen as the new law intended.
- **Spain**, with one of the highest deceased donation rates in the world, has an opt-out law. Yet the leaders of its national transplant organisation argue that there are no clear examples of a sustained increase in donation after any country changed its law, and credit Spain's success to organisation: a trained transplant coordination team in every donor hospital.

**The South African view.** A 2021 review of opt-in and opt-out policies by Harriet Etheredge of Wits Donald Gordon Medical Centre found little difference between the two systems for increasing donor numbers when either is used in isolation, and argued that barriers must be tackled at several levels alongside any change. South Africa's own transplant community has been more direct. The national strategy workshop convened by the Southern African Transplantation Society agreed, as a starting assumption, that with consent rates this low and public understanding of donation this limited, **there is no evidence to support an assumption that most South Africans would consent**. Presumed consent presumes a "yes" that South African families are not, at present, giving.

**What would help, then?** The same answer runs through all of it: the chain. In Johannesburg, the Wits transplant procurement model raised deceased donor numbers by targeting the two points where potential donations were being lost, referral and family consent. Trained coordinators, hospitals that refer every potential donor, families who have heard their relative's wishes, and public understanding: that is where the evidence points, whatever the consent model.

## Scarcity and fairness

**The gap.** At the end of 2021, **2,586 people** were on South Africa's national transplant waiting list. That year, **229 solid organ transplants** were performed, and **189 people died waiting**. These are the most recent verified national figures, from the joint SATS/SATCS report to the World Health Organization's Global Observatory. Every allocation decision is made inside that gap.

**Who has access.** The transplant community's own national strategy is frank about it. Transplant services are concentrated in large cities in the wealthier provinces, and are more easily available to people who live near them and to those who can use private health care. It also records Western Cape figures in which family consent rates in the public sector were far below those in the private sector. So inequity runs both ways: in who is offered a transplant, and in whose families are approached and supported well enough to say yes.

Fairness therefore has two sides:

- **Who is asked.** No community should be left out of the offer to donate. The Western Cape policy says a family's ethnic, cultural or spiritual background must never be used as a reason not to raise donation. Skipping the conversation on an assumption is itself a form of exclusion.
- **Who receives.** Booklet 7 forbids practitioners to let their views about a patient's age, disability, race, culture, beliefs, sexuality, gender, lifestyle or social and economic status prejudice the treatment they offer. The draft transplant regulations of September 2026 would add an explicit rule that allocation may not take a deceased donor's race, religion, culture or similar characteristics into account. They are not yet law.

**Donors who are less than ideal.** Scarcity also shapes which organs are used. The SATCS Red File records that, to address the shortage, extended selection criteria are sometimes applied, and people with conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes or HIV can be considered as donors, with **each organ assessed individually** for whether it can be transplanted. It reports that such donors have shown excellent outcomes, with a significant survival benefit over no transplant. The ethical weight falls on honesty with recipients: an organ that is less than ideal can still be far better than waiting, and the recipient's team is responsible for weighing that and explaining it.

**Money.** South Africa's transplant community holds that donation should be **financially neutral**: neither donors nor their families should lose or gain from it. Its national strategy says outright that paying a donor family's funeral costs is not an ethical answer to the shortage. That is the ethical reasoning behind the legal ban on reward in section 60.
