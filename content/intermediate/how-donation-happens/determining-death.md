## Two ways, one definition

South Africa recognises two ways of determining that someone has died:

- **By neurological criteria**, usually called **brain death**. The brain, including the brainstem, has permanently stopped working, while a ventilator keeps oxygen moving through the body.
- **By circulatory criteria**, or **circulatory death**. The heart has stopped, and circulation and breathing have been absent for long enough that they will not return.

These are not two kinds of death, and neither is a stage on the way to dying. They are two routes to establishing the same fact: the capacity for consciousness and the capacity to breathe have both been lost, irreversibly. Which route applies depends on the circumstances, and in particular on whether a ventilator is involved.

The tests in this lesson come from the *South African guidelines on the determination of death*, written for the Critical Care Society of Southern Africa and published in 2021 in the peer-reviewed *Southern African Journal of Critical Care*. It is South African guidance, written for South African hospitals, and the guideline itself says that, provided every precondition is met, death can be determined with complete certainty.

## Brain death is not a coma

This is the distinction you will be asked to explain most often, and it is the one most often wrong in films and news reports.

**A coma** is profound unconsciousness. The brain is badly injured, but it has not lost all of its function. The brainstem, the part at the base of the brain that controls breathing and the most basic reflexes, still works. People do recover from comas, which is exactly why a coma must never be confused with death.

**Brain death** is the complete and irreversible loss of all brain function, including the brainstem. There is nothing left to recover. The guidelines put it plainly: there is no documented case of anyone who met the preconditions and criteria for brain death ever regaining any brain function.

**So why is the heart still beating?** Because a ventilator is supplying the oxygen that a person who is brain dead can no longer take in for themselves, and with that oxygen the heart can go on beating for a time after death. The rise and fall of the chest is the ventilator's work too. What the family sees at the bedside is the machine, not a sign of life.

## Before any test begins

The most common challenge to brain death is a fair one: *how do you know they aren't just deeply sedated, or very cold?* The answer is that nobody tests for brain death until those possibilities have been ruled out. Several things can make a living brain look, temporarily, as if it has stopped working. The guidelines require each of them to be excluded first:

- **There must be a known cause.** An established injury, such as a severe head injury, a major bleed in the brain or a prolonged loss of oxygen, that is capable of destroying the whole brain permanently. Testing does not start on a patient whose unresponsiveness has no explanation.
- **Body temperature must be normal.** A very cold body can suppress brain function in a way that recovers on warming.
- **Blood pressure must be adequate.** A brain that is not receiving enough blood can stop responding without having died.
- **Sedatives and other drugs that depress the brain must be excluded.** Many patients in intensive care have been given them, so there must be time for them to clear, or proof that they are not having an effect.
- **Severe chemical imbalances in the blood must be corrected.** Some can dull or switch off brain responses on their own.

This step is what makes the tests mean anything. If every condition that could *mimic* brain death has been ruled out, and the brain still shows no function, then the only remaining explanation is the injury itself, and the injury is one that cannot be undone.

## The three-step test

Once the preconditions are met, the test has three parts. Each one closes off a different possibility.

**1. Coma.** The patient is completely unresponsive. Strong pressure produces no grimace and no movement of the face. Some movements can still come from the spinal cord, which works without the brain, and doctors are trained to tell these reflexes apart from a response. Any response that comes from the brain rules out brain death.

**2. No brainstem reflexes, checked one pathway at a time.** The brainstem carries a set of automatic reflexes that still work in a deep coma. Each one tests a different set of nerves running through the brainstem, so checking them one by one tests the brainstem as a whole, not just one spot:

- a bright light shone into each eye, to see whether the pupils react;
- the surface of the eye (the cornea) touched, to see whether the eye blinks;
- firm pressure just above the eye, to see whether the face grimaces;
- ice-cold water run into each ear, which in a working brainstem makes the eyes move;
- the back of the throat touched, to see whether the patient gags;
- the airway deep in the chest stimulated, to see whether the patient coughs.

If even one of these reflexes is present, the brainstem is still working and the patient is not brain dead.

**3. The apnoea test.** *Apnoea* means the absence of breathing. Because the loss of the capacity to breathe is part of the definition of death, it is tested directly rather than assumed. Under controlled conditions, the patient is disconnected from the ventilator while oxygen continues to be supplied, and the doctors watch to see whether the body ever tries to take a breath. As the body goes without breathing, the waste gas that normally drives us to breathe builds up in the blood. It is the strongest signal to breathe there is. If no breath comes, the breathing centre in the brainstem has stopped working.

Together the three steps rule out every alternative. The preconditions exclude the conditions that could mimic death. The coma and brainstem tests show that no part of the brain is responding. The apnoea test shows that the one function the body cannot live without has gone. That is why a completed determination is final.

## Who makes the determination

Brain death is determined by **at least two doctors**. At least one of them must have been practising for **five years or more** since registering, and **none of them may be involved in the transplant**. The guidelines recommend that they examine the patient together, and strongly advocate that the apnoea test be done once, by both doctors at the same time.

The time of death is the time at which the determination is complete, with every required test done. It is not the moment the ventilator is later switched off, and it is not the moment donation is discussed.

## Circulatory death

Circulatory death is determined differently, because the situation is different. Here the heart has stopped.

Death can only be determined this way in one of three situations: trying to restart the heart would be inappropriate, attempts to restart it have failed, or treatment aimed at keeping the patient alive has been withdrawn. In the donation setting it is the third. The guideline lists the reasons treatment may be withdrawn: it can no longer benefit the patient and continuing it is not in their best interests, or the patient refused it in an advance directive, or their legal decision-maker has done so on their behalf.

Once the heart has stopped, the doctor observes the patient for at least **five minutes**, during which there must be a **continuous** absence of circulation and of breathing. At the end of that period the doctor confirms that the pupils do not react to light and that there is no movement in response to pressure above the eye. The time of death is the moment those criteria are met.

**Why the wait exists.** A heart that has stopped can, very rarely, start again on its own in the first minutes. So the guideline sets five continuous minutes as the period that establishes the stopping is irreversible, and adds a rule: if circulation or breathing returns at any point, the five minutes start again from zero. The wait is there to make certain. It is not a countdown run for the transplant team's benefit. Without the reason the wait can look arbitrary, or worse, so knowing it is what lets you answer the question honestly.

Two more rules close the gap people worry about. Once death has been confirmed, nothing may be done that could restore blood flow to the brain. And where organs are to be donated, a **second doctor** must also certify the death: as with brain death, at least one of the two must have more than five years' experience, and neither may be involved with the transplant team.

One more point matters here, and later Stages return to it in depth. In donation after circulatory death, nothing starts until a decision to withdraw treatment has been made **for the patient's own reasons**, by the team caring for them, independently of any question of donation. Donation is only considered once that decision stands on its own.

## Putting the two side by side

Brain death is determined by showing that the brain has permanently stopped working, while a ventilator keeps the heart beating. The organs go on receiving blood and oxygen after death, and that is what makes **donation after brain death (DBD)** possible.

Circulatory death is determined by showing that the circulation has permanently stopped. From the moment the heart stops, the organs are without blood. That is why **donation after circulatory death (DCD)** runs under much greater time pressure, and why what can be donated depends on how quickly recovery can follow.

Both determinations are careful, rule-bound and done by doctors who have nothing to do with any transplant. Each establishes, by its own route, that the death cannot be reversed.
