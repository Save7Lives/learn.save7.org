A donation is not one decision at one moment. It is a chain of steps, run by different people over many hours, and it can break at almost any link. This lesson walks the chain in order.

## 1. Identification: someone has to think of it

Organ donation can only happen in a hospital, in practice an intensive care unit, from a patient on a ventilator. So the chain starts with the doctors and nurses already caring for that patient. **Identifying a possible donor is the treating team's responsibility**, as part of ordinary end-of-life care. Nobody else is in the room to do it.

The guidance used by South African transplant coordinators names three situations that should prompt the thought, for any patient on a ventilator:

- a decision has been made to test for brain death;
- the patient has a catastrophic brain injury that sedation cannot explain, and there has been time to judge the outlook;
- the team intends to discuss withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, and death is expected to follow.

None of these is a decision to donate. Each is simply a point at which donation might be possible, and at which someone should ask.

## 2. Referral: early, and before the family is asked

At that point the treating team contacts a **transplant coordinator**, or a **tissue donation coordinator** where only tissue is possible. The South African guidelines on the determination of death set the timing: the assessment of donation potential, with the coordinator, should happen when the team decides to test for brain death or to begin discussing withdrawal of treatment with the family. In other words, **before end-of-life matters are raised with the family**, not after. The same guidelines treat exploring a patient's wishes about donation as a standard part of end-of-life care.

Timing matters for two reasons:

- **The coordinator can find out whether donation is possible at all.** Screening often shows that it is not. Finding that out early means the family is never asked about something that cannot happen.
- **Support takes time to arrange.** An early call lets the coordinator plan who will be involved and when, so the family is not kept waiting later.

**There is no legal duty to refer.** The steps that follow a referral are tightly regulated, but whether the call is made at all depends on a clinician choosing to make it. So a donation that was possible can be lost without anyone ever deciding against it, simply because nobody asked. (Draft regulations published for comment in September 2026 would change this for hospitals that carry out donation. They are not yet law, and Stage 3 covers them.)

## 3. Determination of death

Death is determined by one of the two routes in the previous lesson: by neurological criteria, for a patient on a ventilator, or by circulatory criteria, after treatment has been withdrawn. Either way, the doctors who determine it have nothing to do with any transplant.

## 4. The family is told, and only then asked

The family is first told that their relative has died, and is given time to understand and accept it. **Donation is not raised in the same conversation.** It comes later, and it is usually raised by the coordinator rather than by the doctor who delivered the news.

This separation is called **decoupling**. It means a family is never asked to absorb a death and make a donation decision in the same breath, and never hears the news of a death as a request. Stage 2 covers the conversation itself, and who has the right to consent.

## 5. Consent and authorisation

Consent comes from the family, in an order set by law. Where the death was unnatural, such as a road accident, the Forensic Pathology Service must also authorise what may be recovered, and the hospital gives its own written authorisation. Stages 2 and 3 cover each of these.

## 6. Donor management

After brain death, the body begins to deteriorate, because the brain no longer regulates things such as temperature. Care therefore continues after death, and it is active: breathing, circulation, temperature and fluids are all managed. **Active management increases the number of organs that can be recovered and improves how well recipients do.** It is treatment with a purpose, not holding care.

Families can usually stay with their relative during this time, up to the point of transfer to theatre.

## 7. Recovery

Organs are recovered in an operating theatre, as a surgical procedure performed with the same care and respect as any other operation. Tissue is recovered by trained tissue recovery technicians. Afterwards the body is restored to its original appearance.

## 8. Allocation

Organs are matched to recipients. Allocation is a matching process, not a queue in which the person who has waited longest goes first.

## Two organ pathways: DBD and DCD

Most deceased organ donation follows one of two pathways. They differ at almost every step.

**Donation after brain death (DBD).** The patient is on a ventilator, and death is determined by neurological criteria. Because the ventilator keeps the heart beating, the organs go on receiving blood after death. There is time for donor management and for recovery to be planned. The heart, lungs, liver, kidneys and pancreas can all be considered, as well as tissue.

**Donation after circulatory death (DCD).** The team caring for the patient has made an independent decision, in line with palliative care, to withdraw life-sustaining treatment. Only then is donation considered. If the family consents, support continues while the retrieval is prepared, and then treatment is withdrawn with two doctors on hand to certify death. Nothing is done to hasten the dying. Once death has been determined, recovery has to begin as soon as possible, because the organs are damaged quickly once they have no blood supply. So what can be donated depends on how quickly that happens.

Sometimes the heart does not stop within the time the organs can tolerate. The recovery team then stands down, the patient's palliative care continues with the treating team, and the family is told that donation was not possible. The Western Cape's 2025 donation policy, which sets out these steps, describes DCD as still relatively unfamiliar in South Africa, and most deceased organ donation here follows the DBD pathway.

## Organs and tissue: two different pathways

**Organs** have to keep working. They can only come from a DBD or DCD donor, in hospital, and must be transplanted within hours. They are not stored.

**Tissue**, such as corneas, bone, skin and heart valves, does not have to keep working in the same way. It can be donated irrespective of the manner of death, it can be recovered hours or even days after death, including after the body has been moved to a mortuary, and it does not need a ventilator at all. Tissue is processed and stored by a **tissue bank**, so it does not have to be matched to a recipient that day.

That is why far more people can donate tissue than can donate organs.

## Who's who

- **Organ Donor Foundation (ODF).** A non-profit founded in 1988 as the national umbrella body for promoting organ and tissue donation. It runs awareness campaigns and the donor register. It is not a hospital and plays no part in decisions at the bedside.
- **Southern African Transplantation Society (SATS).** The professional society for transplantation in the region. With SATCS it reports South Africa's national donation and transplant figures, and it convened the workshop behind the national strategy roadmap for organ and tissue donation.
- **South African Transplant Coordinators Society (SATCS).** Founded in 2017 as a special-interest group of SATS, to educate, develop and support transplant coordinators. It publishes the *Organ and Tissue Donation Reference File*, known as the Red File, the coordinators' own reference.
- **Transplant coordinators.** Usually specialist nurses. They are the key point of contact between the bereaved family, the clinical team and the transplant team: they take the referral, screen the potential donor, oversee donor management, approach the family about donation and coordinate everything from referral to recovery.
- **Tissue banks.** Organisations that recover, process, store and supply donated tissue. South Africa's include cornea and eye banks, a heart valve bank and a multi-tissue bank for bone, skin and corneas.
- **Transplant centres.** The hospital units, public and private, where transplants are performed. The SATS roadmap counts 21 transplant centres, including the tissue banks above, and notes that they are concentrated in large cities in the wealthier provinces. Stage 4 returns to what that means for fairness.
