## The definition

Death is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness together with the irreversible loss of the capacity to breathe. It is determined by neurological criteria (brain death) or by circulatory criteria (circulatory death). A correctly performed clinical examination, with every precondition met, determines death with complete certainty.

Terms worth knowing:

- **Brain death**, or death by neurological criteria, is the preferred term, rather than *brain-stem death*.
- **Circulatory death**, or death by circulatory criteria, is preferred over *cardiac* or *cardiorespiratory death*.
- **Somatic support** is the continued management of the body and organs, other than the brain, after brain death has been confirmed.

## Brain death: preconditions

Testing may only begin once all of these are established:

- an established cause compatible with complete and irreversible loss of all brain function;
- normal body temperature;
- adequate blood pressure;
- the effect of sedatives and other drugs that depress the brain excluded;
- severe metabolic, chemical and hormonal disturbances corrected.

Their purpose is to exclude every condition that could mimic brain death.

## Brain death: the three steps

1. **Coma.** No response of the face to strong pressure, and no movement other than reflexes coming from the spinal cord.
2. **Absent brainstem reflexes**, each tested separately: pupils to a bright light, blink when the cornea is touched, grimace to pressure above the eye, eye movement when ice-cold water is run into the ear, gag, and cough.
3. **The apnoea test.** Disconnected from the ventilator under controlled conditions, with oxygen still supplied, the body makes no attempt to breathe despite the strongest possible signal to do so.

The guidelines recommend that the two doctors test together, with a single apnoea test done by both. The time of death is when the determination is complete.

## Circulatory death

Possible only where attempting to restart the heart would be inappropriate, where resuscitation has failed, or where treatment aimed at sustaining life has been withdrawn. In the donation setting it is the last of these.

- At least five minutes of continuous absence of circulation and breathing, observed by the doctor.
- Any return of circulation or breathing restarts the five minutes.
- At the end: no pupil response to light, and no movement in response to pressure above the eye.
- Time of death is recorded when those criteria are met.
- Nothing that could restore blood flow to the brain may be started once death is confirmed.

## Who determines death

At least two medical practitioners where organs are to be donated. At least one must have practised for five years or more since registration, and none may take part in the transplant. The legal source of that rule is Regulation 9 of the 2012 regulations under the National Health Act, covered in Stage 3.

## The chain

Identification (the treating team) → referral (early, before the family is approached; not a legal duty) → determination of death → the family is told → donation raised separately (decoupling) → consent and authorisations → donor management → recovery → allocation.

## DBD and DCD

- **DBD.** A ventilated patient is determined dead by neurological criteria. Organs are still perfused, so there is time for donor management and planning. Heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, pancreas and tissue can all be considered.
- **DCD.** Follows an independent decision by the treating team to withdraw treatment. Donation is considered only afterwards. Nothing is done to hasten death. Recovery must begin as soon as possible after death is certified. If the patient does not die within a pre-agreed time, the recovery team stands down and palliative care continues.

## Organs and tissue

Organs must keep working, come only from DBD or DCD donors in hospital, and are transplanted within hours. Tissue can be donated irrespective of the manner of death, recovered hours or days after death including from a mortuary, needs no ventilator, and is stored by tissue banks.

## Who's who

The ODF (awareness and the donor register), SATS (the professional society for transplantation), SATCS (the transplant coordinators' society, publisher of the Red File), transplant coordinators (usually specialist nurses who run the process from referral to recovery), tissue banks, and the transplant centres, concentrated in the large cities.

## Deliberately not taught

Clinical values and thresholds, the additional tests used when a clinical examination cannot be completed, and the special provisions for children, pregnancy and patients on heart-lung machines. The SATCS Red File's bedside summary includes an atropine test; the guideline does not require it, and it is not a fourth step.
