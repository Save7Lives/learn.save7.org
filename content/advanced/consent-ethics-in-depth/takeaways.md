- **Two decisions, two moments.** Treatment decisions for a dying patient are made under section 7 of the National Health Act and the HPCSA's consent guidance. Donation after death is decided under section 62. They are separate decisions, and the conversation must keep them separate.

- **The two family lists are different.** Section 7: a written mandate or a legally authorised person, then spouse or partner, then parent, grandparent, adult child, brother or sister. Section 62: spouse, partner, major child, parent, guardian, major brother, major sister.

- **A proxy for treatment is not a proxy for donation.** The person a patient appointed in writing to make health decisions has no special standing under section 62.

- **An advance directive carries professional weight, not statutory force.** The HPCSA requires an applicable, competent refusal of treatment to be respected, but no Act makes a living will binding. The 1998 Law Commission bill and a 2019 private member's bill both came to nothing.

- **Best interests is about the patient.** Clinical options, the patient's previously expressed wishes, their background and values, and the least restrictive option are what count. What the family would choose for themselves is not the test.

- **Withdrawal stands on its own.** The treating team decides independently to withdraw treatment, and only then is donation considered. Nothing is done to hasten death, and the dead donor rule holds.

- **The person's own decision comes first.** A family may donate only in the absence of the person's own donation or a contrary direction they gave while alive. A considered refusal is the law, not just good manners.

- **Valid consent is capable, informed and free, not just signed.** Distress is not incapacity. A family must know what is material to them. No pressure, guilt or haste.

- **Consent is a process.** Verbal consent is followed by written consent, and a signature on its own proves nothing about understanding.

- **Settle the section 62 order in planning.** The relative who has been deciding about treatment may not be the one whose consent to donation the law recognises.
