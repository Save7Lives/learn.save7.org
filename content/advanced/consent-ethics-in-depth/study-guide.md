## The two decisions

**Before death: consent to treatment.** The patient is alive and cannot decide. Governed by the National Health Act sections 6 to 8 and HPCSA Booklet 4 (*Seeking patients' informed consent*, revised December 2021). The family helps the team understand what the patient would want.

**After death: donation.** Governed by National Health Act section 62. The family, in the order the Act sets, may donate, unless the person donated or refused while alive.

## Section 7: who consents for a patient who cannot

1. A person the patient mandated in writing
2. A person authorised by law or court order, such as a curator
3. The spouse or partner
4. Then a parent, grandparent, adult child, or brother or sister, in that order

Whoever consents must, where possible, consult the patient first (section 8). If capacity is in doubt or a dispute can't be resolved, practitioners consult colleagues and may seek a court ruling.

## Section 62: who may donate after death (recap from Intermediate)

1. The person themselves, while alive, in one of the forms the Act sets out
2. If they did not, and gave no contrary direction: the spouse, partner, major child, parent, guardian, major brother, major sister, in that order
3. If nobody on the list can be found after the prescribed steps: the Director-General, for specific tissue

A contrary direction ends the question. Asking what the person wanted can settle the matter.

## The four differences between the lists

- A written mandate counts under section 7 and has no place in section 62.
- Section 7 puts a parent before an adult child; section 62 puts a major child before a parent.
- Section 7 ranks a spouse and a partner together; section 62 ranks the spouse first.
- A grandparent appears only in section 7; a guardian only in section 62.

## Advance directives

- **What they are:** a competent person's statement of the treatment they would or would not want if they lose capacity.
- **HPCSA position:** find out whether one exists. Respect a competent refusal that clearly applies and hasn't been changed. Otherwise take the patient's known wishes into account. Booklet 7 (revised September 2025) encourages patients to put directives in writing and allows an appropriately drafted living will; the palliative care guidelines (Booklet 17, 2019) list what a directive should cover.
- **Legal status:** no statute gives a living will binding force. The South African Law Commission's 1998 draft *End of Life Decisions Act* was never enacted, and a 2019 private member's bill lapsed. The Act recognises a written mandate (a proxy) under section 7.
- **Clarke v Hurst (1992):** stopping artificial feeding of a patient in a persistent vegetative state was held not unlawful by the community's standards. The court did not rest its order on the living will.

## The best-interests test

For a patient who cannot decide and has no applicable directive or appointed decision-maker, weigh:

- the clinically appropriate options
- what the patient previously said they wanted
- the patient's background: culture, religion, work
- what people who know the patient say about the patient's values
- the option that least restricts the patient's future choices

The family's own preferences are not the test.

## Applying the HPCSA framework to a family's donation decision

**Capacity.** Presumed. Distress, or a decision you disagree with, is not incapacity. Impairment from alcohol or drugs, duress, coercion or manipulation invalidates consent. The remedy for someone struggling to take things in is time and repetition.

**Information.** Disclose what is material: what a reasonable person in the family's position would attach significance to. For donation, that means the process and timing, continued treatment of the donor, that donation may not go ahead, the family's right to set limits, research use of unsuitable tissue, forensic authorisation, and that there is no cost to the family. Use an interpreter where needed.

**Voluntariness.** Recommend, don't pressurise. Declare conflicts of interest. Warm, accurate information is legitimate; guilt, apology and haste are not.

**Process.** Consent is a continuing dialogue, and a signature is not proof of it. Verbal consent is followed by written consent. In McQuoid-Mason's reading, a consenting family may change its mind, but not once a recipient's operation is under way.

## Where end-of-life care meets donation

- The Western Cape policy describes the family, before death, as surrogate decision-makers exploring whether the patient would have wished to donate.
- Withdrawal of treatment follows an independent decision by the treating team on palliative-care grounds.
- Nothing is done to hasten the dying process.
- The dead donor rule applies throughout.
