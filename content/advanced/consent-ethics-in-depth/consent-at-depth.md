## Two decisions, two moments

Picture a patient in ICU with a catastrophic brain injury. Before the story ends, their family is likely to be involved in two decisions.

**The first is made while the patient is alive:** what treatment they should have now that they cannot decide for themselves, including whether life-sustaining treatment should continue. It is a decision about a living person's care. It is governed by informed consent, section 7 of the National Health Act, and the HPCSA's guidance.

**The second is made after death:** whether to donate. It is a decision about what happens to a person's body once they have died. It is governed by section 62 of the Act.

Intermediate Level taught section 62 in full and warned against confusing its list with the one in section 7. This Stage explains why the difference matters so much in practice. The two are not stages of one decision, and treating them as one is the mistake the whole approach is built to avoid. It is why the death and the donation are separate conversations (Stage 2). It is why the doctors who establish death must be independent of transplantation. And it is why, as Intermediate Level showed, donation after circulatory death is considered only after the treating team has decided **independently** to withdraw treatment.

## Before death: when the patient can't decide

### Who decides: section 7 in full

The Act starts from the rule that no health service is provided without the patient's informed consent. When a patient cannot consent, section 7(1) sets out who can consent for them. The family order is only the third step:

1. **a person the patient mandated in writing** to consent on their behalf
2. otherwise, **a person authorised by law or a court order**, such as a curator
3. otherwise, the patient's **spouse or partner**
4. and, in the absence of a spouse or partner, a **parent, grandparent, adult child, or brother or sister**, "in the specific order as listed"

Section 8 adds a duty that is easy to forget. Whoever consents for the patient must, where possible, consult the patient first. And the HPCSA's informed-consent guidance (Booklet 4) says that where capacity is in doubt, or a dispute about the patient's best interests can't be resolved, practitioners should consult experienced colleagues and, if needed, seek a court's ruling.

### Advance directives: what they are, and what they aren't

An **advance directive**, or *living will*, is a statement made while a person is competent about the treatment they would or would not want if they lose capacity. The HPCSA treats it seriously:

- Booklet 4 tells practitioners to find out whether an incapacitated patient mandated someone in writing, or set out their preferences in an advance statement.
- It says practitioners **must respect a refusal of treatment** made while the patient was competent, provided it clearly applies to the present situation and there is no reason to think the patient changed their mind. Where there is no such statement, the patient's known wishes are still taken into account.
- The HPCSA's current guidance on withholding and withdrawing treatment (Booklet 7, revised September 2025) says patients should be encouraged to put their directives for future care in writing, that "an appropriately drafted living will" may be used, and that patients should be able to revise their directives from time to time.
- The palliative care guidelines (Booklet 17, 2019) list what a directive should cover: treatment preferences, the beliefs behind them, who should be involved in decisions, emergency measures such as CPR, preferred place of care, and spiritual needs.

**What an advance directive is not, in South Africa, is a statute.** No Act gives a living will binding legal force.

- The South African Law Commission recommended legislation in 1998, with a draft *End of Life Decisions Act*, and it was never enacted.
- A private member's bill to recognise living wills was introduced in Parliament in February 2019 and lapsed in May the same year.
- What the Act *does* recognise is the written mandate in section 7: a person appointed in writing to decide for you. That is a proxy, not a living will.

In the best-known South African case on withdrawing treatment, *Clarke v Hurst* (1992), the court held that stopping artificial feeding of a patient in a persistent vegetative state was not unlawful. It judged that by the community's standards, and it did not rest its order on the patient's living will.

So an advance directive carries real professional and ethical weight, and it is the clearest evidence there is of what a person wanted. But its weight comes from professional guidance and from what it shows about the person's wishes, not from a statute.

### The best-interests test

Intermediate Level noted that when a patient cannot take part and has left no advance directive, the decision about treatment is taken in the patient's **best interests**. The HPCSA spells out what that means. Practitioners must weigh:

- the options that are clinically appropriate
- any evidence of what the patient previously said they wanted, including an advance statement
- what the team knows of the patient's background, such as their culture, religion and work
- what people who know the patient say about the patient's values and preferences
- where more than one option is reasonable, the one that least restricts the patient's future choices

Notice what is not on the list: what the family would choose for themselves. In a best-interests decision the family's role is to tell the team **who the patient was**. Booklet 7 requires close family to be consulted, and the palliative care guidelines make the practitioner, in consultation with the family, responsible for the decision. Where there is serious disagreement about the patient's best interests, Booklet 7 asks for a clinical or ethical review independent of the team, and only then legal advice about going to court. That is a different job from the one a family does after death.

### Where end-of-life care meets donation

The Western Cape policy treats donation as a normal part of end-of-life care. While the patient is still alive, it describes the family as **surrogate decision-makers** exploring whether the patient would have wished to donate. That fits the HPCSA's test, which counts a patient's known wishes among the things that matter.

What it does not do is let donation drive treatment. Withdrawal follows the treating team's own decision on palliative-care grounds, nothing is done to hasten death, and the **dead donor rule** holds throughout: donation only after death, and never the cause of it.

## After death: the section 62 decision

### Two lists, side by side

After death, section 7 no longer applies and section 62(2) takes over:

- **Section 7 (treatment, while alive):** a written mandate or a person authorised by law, then spouse *or* partner, then parent, grandparent, adult child, brother or sister.
- **Section 62(2) (donation, after death):** spouse, partner, major child, parent, guardian, major brother, major sister.

The differences are small on the page and large in a family:

1. **A written mandate has no place in section 62.** The person a patient appointed to make their health decisions has no special standing over donation, unless they also rank first on the section 62 list.
2. **Children and parents swap places.** Section 7 puts a parent ahead of an adult child; section 62 puts a major child ahead of a parent. A widowed patient's mother may be the right person to consent to treatment, while her adult son is the right person to consent to donation.
3. **A spouse and a partner are ranked together for treatment**, but for donation the spouse comes first.
4. **A grandparent appears only in section 7**, and a guardian only in section 62.

So the person the team has been talking to all week about treatment may not be the person whose consent to donation the law recognises. Nobody should discover that in the middle of the donation conversation. It is one more thing to settle in planning, before the family meeting.

None of this means a coordinator speaks only to the person at the top of the list. FACTS works with all the key family members, and a decision the family cannot live with is a bad outcome, whatever the law allows. But knowing whose consent the law actually recognises is part of getting consent right.

### The person's own decision ends the question

Intermediate Level covered how a person can donate for themselves, and that a person who said no while alive gave a "contrary direction" that removes the family's power to donate. In the conversation, that has a practical meaning: **asking what the person wanted is not a courtesy. It can settle the matter.** A clear refusal is respected because the law requires it, not only because FACTS advises it. A clear wish to donate, even one that doesn't meet section 62's formal requirements, is still the strongest evidence a family has when they are asked what their relative would have wanted.

## The HPCSA framework, applied at depth

Intermediate Level applied the basics of Booklet 4 to a family's donation decision: enough information, in a language they understand, checked for understanding, and free of pressure. Four further tests sharpen what "valid" means.

### Capacity

A grieving relative is presumed capable of deciding. Distress is not incapacity, and neither is a decision the team disagrees with. The booklet is explicit that an apparently irrational choice is not in itself evidence of incapacity.

But consent is not informed if it is given under duress, coercion or manipulation, or by someone whose judgement is impaired, for example by alcohol or drugs. When a relative cannot take in or retain information, the booklet's remedy is time: information in manageable amounts, repeated, with time to reflect before and after deciding. That is exactly what FACTS's time-out breaks and repeated steps provide.

### Material information

"Enough information" has a legal standard behind it. South African courts require practitioners to disclose every **material** risk: anything a reasonable person in the family's position would attach significance to, or that the practitioner should know this particular family would. For donation, that includes some things families are rarely told unless someone thinks to:

- what happens between consent and recovery, including continued treatment of the donor
- that donation may still not take place after consent, and in donation after circulatory death, what happens if death does not come in time
- which organs and tissues they are consenting to, and that they may set limits
- for tissue, that tissue unfit for transplantation may be used for ethical research, which the Red File lists as part of tissue consent
- the extra authorisation needed after an unnatural death
- that the family is not billed for the donation

### Voluntariness

The booklet says practitioners may recommend but must not pressurise, and must declare conflicts of interest. In donation this is the line between FACTS's *positive language* and manipulation. Describing accurately and warmly what donation can do is legitimate. Guilt, pressure, apology or a hurried answer is not. The separation between the treating team and the transplant team, and the independence of the doctors who establish death, are how the conflict of interest is kept out of the room.

### Consent is a process, not a signature

The booklet describes consent as a continuing dialogue, and says a signed form is not proof that informed consent was given or still stands. In donation, verbal consent is followed by written consent. And in the reading of Professor David McQuoid-Mason, writing on Chapter 8 in the *South African Medical Journal*, a family that consented may also change its mind, though not at the last moment, once a recipient's operation is under way.

## What this changes in practice

- Know which decision is on the table. A conversation about withdrawing treatment is not a conversation about donation, and should never sound like one.
- Know, before the meeting, who ranks first under section 62. It may not be the person who has been deciding about treatment.
- Ask about what the person wanted, and treat an advance directive or a clear statement as the strongest evidence there is. A clear refusal ends the donation question.
- Remember that the family's role changes. Before death, they tell the team about the patient. After death, section 62 makes the donation decision theirs.
- Check that consent is capable, informed and free, not just signed. Time, interpreters and silence are how that happens.
