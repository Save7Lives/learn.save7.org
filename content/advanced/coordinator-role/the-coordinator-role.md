## Who the coordinator is

In South African hospitals the role is called the **transplant coordinator**, and the Western Cape's donation policy defines it in one line: a transplant coordinator is a **SNOD, a specialist nurse in organ donation**. Other material uses other names for the same person. The Wits Transplant handbook, which Stage 2 draws on, says *procurement coordinator*, because their work is on the donor side, obtaining organs rather than caring for recipients.

Two related roles are easy to confuse with it:

- **The tissue donation coordinator** handles tissue-only donation: corneas, skin, bone, tendons and heart valves. They receive specialist communication training of their own and can usually be reached around the clock. When a patient is going to be an organ donor, the transplant coordinator raises tissue donation with the family as well, and then refers the donor to a tissue coordinator to arrange the recovery.
- **The donation support physician** is a critical care doctor the policy lets teams call in to help manage a consented donor. They are a resource for the team, not a replacement for the coordinator.

📌 Australia's national guideline calls the equivalent role a *Donation Specialist Nurse*, and the evidence it cites shows why the role exists. In "challenging" conversations, where the patient had not registered and staff rather than the family raised donation, families consented 54% of the time when a Donation Specialist was involved, against 33% with other trained staff and 28% with untrained staff. Those are Australian figures and not a South African result. The South African sources make the same point without numbers: the Red File states that trained coordinators are "more likely to get consent from the family", and the Western Cape policy says the consent rate reflects *both* the coordinator and the treating team.

## Brought in before the family is told

Everything else in this Stage depends on timing. Intermediate Level named the three **clinical triggers** that should prompt a call to the transplant coordinator about a ventilated patient. As a reminder, they are:

1. a decision has been made to perform brain death tests
2. there is a catastrophic brain injury with the deepest level of unconsciousness (a Glasgow Coma Scale of 4 or less), not explained by sedation
3. the team intends to discuss with the family withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, after which death is expected

What matters at this Level is the order, and what the order protects. The Western Cape policy is explicit: the first discussion with the coordinator **should happen before end-of-life care is raised with the family at all.** It gives four reasons:

- **It prevents premature conversations.** Nobody mentions donation to a family whose relative turns out not to be a candidate.
- **Screening comes first.** Some referrals are screened out as unsuitable, and it is far kinder to find that out before anyone has raised hopes.
- **The national priority list can be checked.** Some patients on it have agreed to accept organs that others would decline, which widens who could be helped.
- **The donor register can be checked.** The coordinator finds out whether the patient registered with the Organ Donor Foundation before the family is asked anything.

Two rules protect that order. Life-sustaining treatment should not be withdrawn or limited until the donation question has been clarified with the patient's family by, or with, a coordinator. The Wits Transplant Do's and Don'ts put the same rule as a *don't*: do not withdraw ventilation or stop treatment until the patient's donation potential has been assessed. And when a coordinator accepts a referral, they tell the referring team that **nobody should say the words "organ donation" to the family** until the planned approach.

**Referral is not a legal requirement**, as Intermediate Level noted. It is a professional one. Responsibility for referring a potential donor "lies with the entire medical team", it is "an expected part of end-of-life care", and it extends beyond the treating doctor to nursing staff and ancillary healthcare workers. Referrals and their outcomes belong in the patient's notes.

## Where the coordinator sits on the pathway

South Africa's national strategy work uses a shared vocabulary for how a patient becomes a donor. It is adapted from an international framework (Domínguez-Gil and colleagues, 2011) and it shows who is responsible at each step:

1. **Possible donor.** A patient at the end of life, supported in a way that allows donation to be an option. The treating team must identify and refer them.
2. **Potential donor.** Identified and referred in time. The transplant team must formally assess them.
3. **Eligible donor.** Assessed as meeting the medical criteria. Consent must be obtained after a planned approach, by the treating team and the transplant team **together**.
4. **Actual donor.** Consent given and recovery undertaken, by the recovery team.
5. **Utilised donor.** At least one organ transplanted, by the recipients' transplant teams.

Every step is a place where a donation can be lost. The coordinator's work starts at the second step and runs to the end. A donation lost at the first step is lost on the ward before anyone calls, which is why the triggers matter so much. Beneath the whole pathway sits the **dead donor rule**: a patient may become a donor only after death, and recovering organs must never be what causes it.

## The duty chain, end to end

The coordinator's work runs in six parts. Each is a separate responsibility, and the last three are the ones people tend to forget.

### 1. Screening

The coordinator assesses whether donation is feasible. They review the clinical information, check the donor register and the priority list, and work out which organs and tissues might be possible. Where donation is not feasible, the family never hears about it, which is exactly why screening comes first.

### 2. Planning

Before anyone meets the family, the approach is planned with the people who will be in the room: the treating doctor, the nursing sister, and, where they are needed, a trauma counsellor, an interpreter or a faith representative. The planning settles who says what, when, where and in which language. Stage 2 covers this in detail, as the first step of Wits FACTS.

### 3. The conversation

The treating team keeps its own job. The Red File is clear that the primary treatment team remains responsible for giving the family the diagnosis and prognosis. The coordinator leads the conversation in which donation is raised, and consent is obtained by the coordinator wherever possible. The Western Cape policy asks the treating team to stay for the first part of that meeting, so they can correct any misunderstanding left over from earlier conversations. Stage 2 covers how the conversation is actually conducted.

### 4. Authorisation and logistics

Family consent is necessary, and it is not sufficient. After it, the coordinator secures the rest:

- **Forensic authorisation.** Where a death meets the criteria for a forensic post-mortem, the coordinator obtains authorisation from the **Forensic Pathology Service** before any recovery. The forensic pathologist may set limits on what is recovered. If the on-call pathologist declines, the policy expects the coordinator to make sure the decision was discussed with senior cover.
- **The hospital's authorisation.** The medical practitioner in charge of clinical services at the hospital where recovery takes place must authorise it in writing. The Western Cape policy calls this person the *medical manager*, and has them check that family consent and, where needed, forensic authorisation are in place. A refusal here is also escalated, and it is reviewed afterwards.
- **Donor care and theatre.** The treating team continues to manage the donor; only for a consented brain-dead donor may the transplant team legally take over that management. Organ recovery is treated as an emergency case with priority over elective surgery, and **the coordinator is present throughout the recovery operation.** In donation after circulatory death, if the patient does not die within a pre-agreed period (usually about an hour), the theatre team stands down and the treating team continues palliative care. The family was told at consent that this could happen.
- **Tissue.** The tissue donation coordinator arranges tissue recovery directly with the place it will happen: a hospital, a funeral home, a mortuary or a forensic pathology facility.

### 5. Aftercare

The coordinator's responsibility to the family does not end in theatre:

- a family who asked to see their relative after the donation is given time with them, in a suitable place arranged by the coordinator with theatre or ICU staff
- the family's spokesperson is kept updated, and told which organs were transplanted and which could not be used, because uncertainty is hard to grieve through
- the coordinator arranges **anonymous** thank-you letters to the donor family and offers bereavement support where it is wanted
- a family that **declined** is supported too; support does not depend on the answer

There is a balance to keep. A family often wants to know about the people who received the organs. The coordinator tells them enough to answer that, but not enough to identify a recipient, which is easier to do than it sounds in the age of social media. Stage 4 covers the privacy rules behind this.

### 6. Audit

The coordinator reviews the cases that worked and the ones that didn't:

- after every referral, successful or not, the coordinator goes back to the referring staff for feedback and a debrief
- **every missed potential organ donor is audited** and discussed at the unit's morbidity and mortality meeting, and the unit gets feedback and training
- a medical manager's refusal of a donation is reviewed at those meetings too
- a hospital organ and tissue donation committee oversees the policy, and in the Western Cape a provincial committee reports on it annually
- because donation after circulatory death is still unfamiliar, the coordinator leads training and a debrief after **every** case

## The professional body: SATCS

South Africa's transplant coordinators have a professional society: the **South African Transplant Coordinators Society (SATCS)**. It was founded on 30 June 2017 as a special interest group of the Southern African Transplantation Society (SATS). Its members work to a Code of Conduct, and its stated aim is to educate, develop and support every transplant coordinator in the country.

SATCS also produced the **Organ and Tissue Donation Reference File**, known as the Red File. It is a quick-reference guide meant to be kept in every emergency department and ICU in the country, and much of this Level is sourced from it.

SATCS is a professional society, not a regulator, and membership is voluntary. It sets standards for its members; it does not license anyone. Coordinators in South Africa are usually experienced nurses, often from intensive care. The SATCS Code of Conduct binds its members to the codes they already answer to under the Nursing Act and the HPCSA. Registering and disciplining nurses is the job of the **South African Nursing Council**, the statutory regulator under the Nursing Act 33 of 2005.

## What this asks of you

If you work on a ward, the first part of the chain is yours as much as anyone's. Know the three triggers. Make the call early, before end-of-life care is discussed with the family. Record it in the notes. And never mention donation to the family while you are doing it.

If you don't work on a ward, the role is still worth knowing precisely. Families are approached about donation by a coordinator working with the treating team, after a planned approach. They are not approached by a volunteer, an advocate or a well-meaning bystander. Being able to say who does what, and when, is one of the most reassuring things an advocate can offer.
