## Where FACTS comes from

**Wits Transplant FACTS** is described by its authors as a programme of structured communication for approaching families and asking for consent to deceased organ donation. It adapts the NHS Blood and Transplant strategy *Approaching the Families of Potential Organ Donors*, "modified to suit our South African healthcare setting". It is set out in the *Wits Transplant Procurement Handbook* (2019), and SATCS reproduces that section in the Red File.

The authors describe FACTS as **an eight-step process**, and they are candid that no family is exactly like the last. The strategy expects a coordinator to adjust it to the family, the hospital and their own personality. What they insist on is the underlying sequence.

FACTS began in January 2018 as one procurement coordinator's quality-improvement project at Wits Donald Gordon Medical Centre. The team published her results in the *South African Medical Journal* in 2019. Her own consent rate rose from 25% before FACTS to 73% after it, 35 of the 48 families she approached. The authors call it a "proof of concept", and they are right to: it is one coordinator at one centre, compared before and after, not a controlled trial. Even so, it points the same way as the Australian figures in Stage 1: how the conversation is conducted changes what families decide.

📌 **The same sequence appears abroad.** Australia's national guideline, which describes itself as consistent with the UK guidance FACTS adapts, sets out five elements: routine referral, communicating end-of-life, planning the approach, discussing donation, and reviewing practice. Closer to home, the Western Cape policy's own family-approach appendix is adapted from the 2013 edition of the NHSBT guidance. The five elements are Australian, not South African practice, but the fact that separate systems arrived at the same arc is worth knowing: **plan, separate the death from the donation, offer donation together, then review.** FACTS is South Africa's version of that arc, in eight steps.

## The eight steps

### Step 1: Planning

Planning starts the moment a potential donor is referred. The coordinator gathers the clinical information, confirms with the transplant team that donation is possible, and brings the treating team into the plan so that everyone agrees who will do what, when and where. If anyone on the treating team is opposed to donation, the handbook's advice is that their contact with the family should be limited as far as possible.

Four questions shape the approach:

1. Who are the key family members, and what are the dynamics between them?
2. Which language will they understand best, and is an interpreter needed?
3. Are there cultural or religious practices that matter to them, and should a faith representative be there?
4. When are the key family members usually all present?

On the third question the handbook is emphatic: an apparent cultural or religious obstacle is **not** a reason to hold back, and exploring it sensitively has changed families' decisions. Interpreters matter in South Africa more than almost anywhere. They should be chosen carefully, ideally someone with a medical background whom the family trusts, and involved from the start of planning with a clearly defined role.

The meeting itself is held in a private room away from the bedside, never as a conversation in the corridor, with the key family members invited by the treating team.

**The principle:** most of a hard conversation's outcome is decided before it starts.

### Step 2: Breaking bad news

The **treating doctor leads** and tells the family that their relative has died. For brain death the handbook's phrase is the whole point: *brain death is death*. The doctor must leave no doubt and no room for false hope, which in practice means a warning that bad news is coming, plain language instead of medical terms, pauses, and scans or diagrams to show the extent of the injury. Some families are offered the chance to see the brainstem death tests at the bedside. Some accept and some decline, and both are fine.

Two things are deliberately absent from this meeting. Donation is **not mentioned**, unless the family raises it themselves. And the coordinator is introduced **by name only**, as part of the team that supports families, never as "the transplant coordinator". At this point the family is not ready for donation, and avoiding the words is a form of respect.

If a family member says "life support", or talks about the machines keeping their relative alive, that is a sign they have not yet understood that brain death is final. The team corrects it gently and at once: the machine is supplying oxygen to the organs, and their relative has already died.

**The principle:** the family must first understand, and then accept, that the death has happened. Everything after this depends on it.

### Step 3: Time-out break

The family is given privacy and as much time with their relative as they need. The handbook calls this break **decoupling**, and warns that people routinely underestimate how long a family needs to accept a death.

The treating team has a job during the break too: it must not undo Step 2. Staff who talk as if there is still hope confuse a family that is trying to accept the death, which is why this belongs in the planning, before it happens.

**The principle:** the death and the donation are two separate conversations, with real time between them.

### Step 4: Assessing understanding and acceptance

In a second meeting, the coordinator or trauma counsellor checks whether each key family member has understood and accepted the death. The best way is to ask the family to tell the story: what happened, what the doctor said, what it meant to them, who their relative was. The team listens to the words they use.

A relative who says *it's just her body in that bed now* has usually accepted the death. One who insists *he will wake up and walk out of here* has not. When someone hasn't, the team keeps supporting them and repeats Steps 2 and 3 if it has to. Family members often reach acceptance at different times.

**The principle:** having been told is not the same as understanding. Check before building on it.

### Step 5: The consent conversation

Only now does the coordinator raise donation. The handbook's advice is to approach it as though none of the family knows anything about it, and to explain what donation involves, what happens between consent and recovery, and when and where recovery takes place. Then the coordinator listens, because concerns that go unheard at this stage tend to come back later as the reasons for a refusal.

The checklist is specific:

- be compassionate, and describe donation in positive terms
- tell the family plainly that **there is no wrong answer**
- assure them that the focus is always the care and dignity of their relative, whether or not donation happens, and that someone will stay with their relative until the recovery is complete
- explain the extra legal steps if the death was unnatural
- use **no** negative, apologetic, manipulative or coercive language

The handbook gives examples of what not to say. One is apologising for having to ask because hospital policy requires it. Another tells a family it is to blame for the death and should at least agree to donation. A third says donation is simply "the right thing to do".

**The principle:** donation is offered, not pushed. A decision made under pressure is not the family's decision, and Stage 3 looks at why the law and professional ethics take that line so seriously.

### Step 6: Time-out break

Once donation has been raised, the coordinator gives the family their contact details and leaves them alone to discuss it. They wait at the nurses' station rather than in the room.

**The principle:** a decision made in front of the person who asked for it is not a free one.

### Step 7: Final family discussion

The family gives its decision. Verbal consent is followed by written consent. Any limits the family sets are respected, whether on which organs and tissues may be donated or on how the recovery is done. The coordinator explains the donor pause and invites the family to take part. They also explain that donation can still fall through after consent, so it is not a shock if it does. Support continues whatever the decision.

A first "no" can be explored, gently, because it may rest on a misunderstanding, missing information or a remark taken out of context. A family worried about what surgery will do to the body can be told, truthfully, that their relative cannot feel anything, that they will be treated with care throughout, and that the body will not be disfigured. But if the person had seriously and deliberately decided against donation, **that decision is respected.** Exploring a refusal means making sure it is informed, not wearing it down.

**The principle:** the aim is a decision the family is at peace with, not a yes.

### Step 8: Follow-up, feedback and support

Support continues for families who declined as well as for those who consented. The referring staff are told the outcome and thanked. A consenting family's spokesperson is kept informed through the recovery and told which organs were transplanted and which were not. The handbook notes how much it helps a grieving family to know, in general terms, where the gift went. Special requests are honoured: something to go with their relative to theatre, or the body returned in time for a weekend funeral.

A thank-you letter follows. The coordinator balances what they share: enough to answer the family's questions, but not enough to identify a recipient. Recipients are encouraged to write anonymous thank-you letters of their own.

**The principle:** the family is not a means to an organ. The relationship does not end when the answer is given.

## The Do's and Don'ts, and which are yours

The handbook sums up the eight steps as a one-page list of Do's and Don'ts. Some of them belong only to the coordinator. Many belong to everyone who is near the family, and those are the ones this Stage asks you to apply.

**Anyone on the treating side, including nurses and allied staff:**

- **Do** refer a potential donor to the transplant coordinator as early as possible, and involve them from the start.
- **Don't** withdraw ventilation or stop treatment until the patient's donation potential has been assessed.
- **Don't** give a family misleading information about their relative's condition.
- **Don't** say "organ donation" or "transplant" to a family that has not yet accepted the death.
- **Don't** say "life support", or that the machines are keeping their relative alive. After brain death, there must be no doubt that the patient has died.
- **Don't** use medical jargon when you explain.
- **Don't** give a family the diagnosis of death and raise donation in the same meeting.
- **Don't** stop supporting the family, whatever they decide.

**The coordinator's, working with the treating team:**

- **Do** plan the approach, introduce yourself by name as part of the team, and let the doctor break the news.
- **Do** confirm that every key family member understands and accepts the death before raising donation.
- **Do** raise donation with compassion and in positive terms, and answer questions knowledgeably.
- **Don't** use negative, apologetic, manipulative or coercive language.
- **Don't** expect an answer immediately; give the family time alone.
- **Do** explore a first refusal gently, obtain written consent, and follow up with the family and the referring hospital.

## When it goes wrong: the two hard cases

The handbook's authors describe two situations they meet repeatedly, even when every step has been followed.

### "We can't be the ones to switch off the machine"

Some families believe the donation conversation is really a way of asking them to decide to turn off the ventilator. It is neither their role nor fair to ask it of them, and the coordinator corrects it: their relative has already died, *brain death is death*, and **the family does not have to make that decision.** The machines will be switched off because their relative has died. They have been left on only to give the family time to say goodbye and to consider donation.

That takes a weight off the family: they can consider donation without feeling that their answer ends their relative's life. It is also completely understandable that families feel it. A relative on a ventilator is still warm, their heart is beating, and when the machine stops they will grow cold.

### A family that disagrees

South African families are often large, and they don't always agree. The handbook's advice is **not** to leave them to argue it out indefinitely, but to help them reach a decision. It says to explore the disagreement sensitively, especially where it comes from a misunderstanding. It also says to bring the conversation back to the person: this decision is about what *they* would have wanted, not about older conflicts in the family. The coordinator does not take sides.

## The donor pause

The **donor pause** is a silent prayer or moment of contemplation, often used in South Africa, to respect the donor and their family, to thank them, and to acknowledge the donor's life. There are usually two: one in ICU, which the family is invited to attend at a time that suits them, and one in theatre before recovery begins. It may be a minute or two of silence, a song sung by the nursing staff, or a short reading.

Tissue donation has its own ways of honouring donors too: a pause before recovery, tribute ceremonies, letters and small commemorative gifts, and memorial walls or gardens.

## Time: what the evidence does and doesn't say

Families decide at different speeds. A slow decision can feel like a refusal forming, and a team under pressure may be tempted to push, or to give up. The evidence is against both.

📌 **Han and colleagues (2017)** studied the families of 107 brain-dead potential donors at one hospital in South Korea:

- **15 families (14%) took 48 hours or more** to decide, and the other 92 decided sooner.
- The slower families consented **73% of the time (11 of 15)**, against **55% (51 of 92)** for the faster ones.
- The authors' conclusion was that a delayed decision was **not inferior**. The difference was not statistically significant, so the study does not show that delay *raises* consent. It shows that delay did not lower it.
- They read the delay as time a family reasonably needs to decide, not as a sign of opposition, and recommended **extended, repeated counselling** while the team keeps the organs viable.

Read it with its limits: one centre, a slower group of only fifteen families, and a different country, legal system and culture. It is illustrative, not South African data.

The same study also shows why patience is not the same as waiting indefinitely. Donation failed after the family had consented in 27% of the slower group, against 16% of the faster group (again not significant). Across the whole study, the commonest reason a consented donation failed was that the donor's condition deteriorated, and in two cases consent came too late. A brain-dead donor's circulation cannot be supported for ever. That is why the handbook asks for a decision before a family leaves the hospital, and why it offers a stalling family another time-out break before returning to them. It is also why the South African guidelines' rule on *accommodation*, which Intermediate Level covered, allows a family time after brain death, but a brief and finite time, explained in advance and ordinarily no more than a day.

The two positions are compatible. **A slow decision is not a refusal, and a family should never be rushed. But time has a clinical cost, so what a family needs is time with support, not pressure and not being left alone to decide.**
