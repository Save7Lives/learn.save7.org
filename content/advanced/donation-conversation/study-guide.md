## FACTS in one line each

1. **Planning.** Starts at referral. The team agrees who does what, and when, where and in which language. An interpreter and a faith representative are arranged if needed. The meeting is in a private room, not at the bedside.
2. **Breaking bad news.** The treating doctor says plainly that the patient has died. *Brain death is death.* The coordinator is introduced by name only. Donation is not mentioned.
3. **Time-out break.** Also called decoupling. The family has privacy and time with their relative, and staff say nothing that suggests hope.
4. **Assessing understanding and acceptance.** The family tells the story in their own words, and the team listens for acceptance. If someone hasn't accepted the death, Steps 2 and 3 are repeated.
5. **The consent conversation.** The coordinator raises donation, explains the process and listens. There is no wrong answer, and no coercive or apologetic language.
6. **Time-out break.** The family discusses donation alone, and the coordinator waits outside.
7. **Final family discussion.** The decision is given, and verbal consent is followed by written consent. The family's limits are respected, the donor pause is explained, and a first refusal may be explored.
8. **Follow-up, feedback and support.** Updates and thanks. Families learn which organs were used, without anything that identifies a recipient. Support continues whatever was decided.

## Why the death and the donation are separated

- A family must understand and accept that death has happened before they can consider donation. A decision taken before that is not a real decision.
- A break lets every family member reach the same understanding, contact others and spend time with their relative.
- The Western Cape policy writes the separation in as best practice: prognosis discussions are kept apart from any request for consent.
- The one exception is a family who raises donation themselves. Even then, the handbook treats moving straight to the consent conversation as the exception, not the rule.

## Words to use and to avoid

**Avoid:** "life support"; "the machines are keeping her alive"; medical jargon; any mention of donation before the death is accepted; apologising for asking; blaming the family; telling them donation is the right thing to do.

**Use:** plain language; a warning before bad news; pauses; scans or diagrams to show the injury; open questions such as *can you tell me what happened?*; and, when the time comes, positive and accurate information about donation.

## The two troubleshooting scenarios

**The machine misconception.** The family thinks they are being asked to decide to switch off the ventilator. Correct it: their relative has already died, the machines will be switched off because of that, and the family doesn't have to make that decision.

**A family in disagreement.** Help them reach a decision rather than leaving them to argue it out. Explore misunderstandings, bring the focus back to what the person would have wanted, and don't take sides.

## The donor pause

A silent prayer or contemplation to honour the donor and their family, often used in South Africa. There are usually two: in ICU, with the family invited, and in theatre before recovery. It may be silence, a song or a short reading.

## 📌 Han et al. 2017, read carefully

- One hospital in South Korea, 107 brain-dead potential donors.
- 15 families took 48 hours or more to decide. They consented 73% of the time (11 of 15), against 55% (51 of 92) for faster families. Not significant.
- Conclusion: a delayed decision was **not inferior**. It does not show that delay improves consent.
- Consented donations failed somewhat more often in the slower group, usually because the donor's condition deteriorated. Patience needs support and clinical care alongside it.
- Not South African data. Illustrative only.

## Role boundaries

- **Treating team:** breaks the news of death, stays for the start of the donation conversation, and avoids hopeful or misleading language.
- **Transplant coordinator:** plans the approach, raises donation, obtains consent and follows up.
- **Everyone near the family:** follows the Don'ts and refers early. Organ donation is raised with a family only by the coordinator working with the treating team. Tissue-only donation has its own route, usually through a tissue donation coordinator.
