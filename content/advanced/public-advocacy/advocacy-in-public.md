## Equity: no community excluded

The Western Cape's donation policy has a section headed *Equality and diversity*, and its first sentence sets the rule: no community is to be excluded from being offered the option to donate. The reasoning that follows is worth knowing, because it is what an advocate needs when the rule is challenged.

- **Donation is possible from every community and culture in South Africa.** The policy adds that long-term outcomes improve when organs can be allocated from a large pool.
- **The conversation helps families whatever they decide.** Discussing donation as part of end-of-life care supports a family independently of the decision they reach. Withholding the conversation withholds that too.
- **Every family is owed a fully informed decision.** The policy names an ethical need not to end conversations early, before adequate information has been given. This is why a specialist coordinator leads them: they can give a family as much time as it needs.
- **Referral must be equal too.** Potential donors from every ethnic background are to be referred to the transplant coordinator. It should never be assumed that a patient or family's ethnic, cultural or spiritual background means donation should not be raised. A faith representative or hospital chaplain may be brought in to support the family.

The last point is where inequity usually enters, and it enters as courtesy. A team that decides a family "wouldn't want to be asked" believes it is being sensitive. What it has actually done is make the family's decision for them, before they were given it, on the strength of an assumption about people like them. The Wits Transplant handbook puts the same point in practical terms, from its authors' own experience: apparent cultural or religious obstacles are not a reason to hold back, and exploring them sensitively has changed families' decisions.

Equity in a hospital is mostly a matter of *not skipping anyone*: every potential donor referred, every family offered the conversation, an interpreter where language requires one, and faith support where the family wants it. Equity in public work means going to the communities the system has reached least, which is what the case study at the end of this Stage is about.

## Privacy and the media

### What the law says

Donation in South Africa is protected by a confidentiality rule that applies to **everyone**, not just hospital staff. Regulation 24 of the 2012 regulations under the National Health Act (GN R180) is headed *Prohibition of publication of certain facts*. It forbids anyone to publish or make known any fact through which the identity of:

- a deceased person whose body or tissue was donated
- the person who donated it
- a living person from whom tissue was removed
- the person who consented to that removal

**may possibly be established**, unless consent was given. A second part protects **recipients**: nothing may be published that could identify the recipient of donated tissue without the recipient's written consent, or, if the recipient has died, without their prior written consent or, if they never objected, the written consent of one of the close relatives the regulation lists.

Two words in that rule do a great deal of work. *Any fact* means the rule is not only about names. *Possibly* means the test is not whether someone *was* identified, but whether they *could* be. A post that gives no names but mentions the town, the date, the hospital and the cause of death may identify a donor to everyone who knows the family.

### How hospitals apply it

The Western Cape policy sets out how this works in practice.

- **Publicity for donation itself is welcome.** Hospital and provincial public relations officers promote awareness and registration through every available channel, and the province commits to working proactively with broadcast and print media.
- **The privacy of donor families and recipients is always protected and paramount.** The coordinator arranges anonymous thank-you letters to donor families and offers bereavement support.
- **Hospitals do not publicise donor details.** A family may choose to do so itself.
- **Time must pass before any public relations work.** This keeps publicity away from a family in acute grief, and away from a transplant that may still turn out badly.

The Wits Transplant handbook adds the everyday version. When a coordinator tells a donor family about the people their relative helped, they share enough to answer the family's questions and never enough to identify a recipient. The handbook notes how easily a recipient can be traced once a family posts on social media: there will always be someone who knows someone.

### What that means for an advocate

- **Never identify a donor, a donor family or a recipient without consent**, and remember that details can identify as surely as names can.
- **Consent must come from the right person.** For a recipient, it must be in writing. A donor family's consent to their own story being told does not cover the recipient's side of it.
- **Wait.** A story told too soon can reach a family still in the first days of grief, or celebrate a transplant that later fails.
- **Let the hospital speak about live cases.** Media questions about a particular donation go through the hospital's public relations office and the coordinator, not through a volunteer's account of what they heard.
- **When a family chooses to go public, that is their choice to make.** Support it, and keep protecting the recipient.

None of this makes donation a secret. The Organ Donor Foundation's awareness work reaches millions of South Africans through television, radio, print and social media. The rules protect *people*, not the subject.

## ULUNTU: a case study in culturally attuned advocacy

### What the sources say

**ULUNTU** is the Organ Donor Foundation's campaign aimed at cultural barriers to donation. There are two descriptions of it, and they differ, so both are given here with their dates.

**The Red File** (the SATCS reference file, whose newest references date from 2021) describes the *Uluntu Project* as the ODF's education work:

- "Uluntu", it says, means community. The project exists because many people in the wider community have a **fear-based understanding** of donation, having never been given factual information, and so may say no automatically when asked after a relative's death.
- It starts from the fact that **the current donor pool is not representative of the South African population**, so cultural and traditional barriers, myths and misunderstandings need to be addressed.
- It is carried out by **culturally similar and culturally sensitive messengers** in under-resourced and vulnerable communities: townships, informal settlements and, when funding allows, rural areas.
- The team visits state hospitals and clinics, and schools, where learners are equipped to be drivers of change in their communities and to influence their elders.

**The ODF's own website** (read in September 2026) calls it the *ULUNTU Awareness Campaign*:

- It glosses the isiXhosa word *uluntu* as meaning both "humanity" and "community".
- It says the ODF is "in the process of rolling out" the campaign in vulnerable communities, as a first step towards breaking through cultural barriers in order to **increase donor consents**.
- Its method begins with information-gathering **with transplant coordinators from feeder hospitals**, then baseline surveys in the communities, then community participative methods, grassroots outreach and preventative education.

Neither source is dated precisely enough to say whether the campaign is active today. The website's wording may be older than the site it now sits on, and no dated updates could be found. Treat ULUNTU as an approach the ODF has described, not as a programme with published results. The Source Corpus contains no campaign toolkit or evaluation, and this Stage deliberately stops at framing.

### Why it is worth studying

Read together, the two descriptions show a model of advocacy that differs from mass awareness in five ways.

1. **It diagnoses the problem as information and trust, not unwillingness.** A "no" given out of fear by someone who never had the facts is not a considered refusal. That matters ethically as well as practically. Stage 3's standard for consent is an *informed* decision, and ULUNTU aims at the "informed" part.
2. **The messenger matters as much as the message.** Culturally similar messengers are more likely to be heard than outsiders delivering the same facts. The Western Cape policy makes the same point from inside the hospital, with faith representatives and interpreters.
3. **It goes where people already are**: clinics, hospitals and schools, rather than waiting for people to come to an awareness event.
4. **It aims at consent, not only registration.** Mass campaigns ask people to register. ULUNTU aims at the moment donation is most often lost, a family's refusal, and it links the community work back to the transplant coordinators who will one day have that conversation.
5. **It treats inequity as the starting point.** It begins from the fact that the donor pool doesn't represent the country, which puts it squarely on the Western Cape policy's equity principle: nobody excluded, everybody informed.

### Questions to ask of any campaign, including this one

- **Who is it for, and who designed it with them?**
- **Who delivers the message**, and would the audience trust them?
- **Does it aim at registration, at families' consent, or at both?**
- **How would anyone know whether it worked?** For ULUNTU, the sources describe baseline surveys, but no results are available in them.
- **Does it respect the answer?** Culturally attuned advocacy is about making sure people decide with the facts. It is not a way to get round a community's settled beliefs.

## What this asks of you

- Never pre-decide for a family or a community. Offer the conversation, and bring the support they want to it.
- Never publish anything that could identify a donor, a donor family or a recipient without the right consent, and leave time before you tell any donation story.
- Judge advocacy, including your own, by whether it leaves people **informed and free to decide**, not by how many say yes.
