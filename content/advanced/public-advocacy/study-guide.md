## Equity obligations

From the Western Cape donation policy's section on equality and diversity:

- No community is excluded from being offered the option to donate.
- Donation is possible from every community and culture in South Africa, and a large donor pool improves long-term outcomes.
- Discussing donation helps families whatever they decide.
- Every family is owed a fully informed decision, and conversations are not ended before adequate information has been given.
- Potential donors from every ethnic background are referred to the transplant coordinator.
- Never assume a family's ethnic, cultural or spiritual background means donation should not be raised. A faith representative or chaplain may support the family.
- Transplant coordinators do outreach in all communities and help clinicians understand families' religious and cultural needs.

## Regulation 24 (GN R180, 2012)

*Prohibition of publication of certain facts.* No person may publish or make known any fact through which the identity of any of these **may possibly be established**, unless consent was given:

- a deceased person whose body or tissue was donated
- the donor
- a living person from whom tissue was removed
- the person who consented to that removal

A **recipient's** identity may not be published unless:

- a living recipient consented in writing, or
- a recipient who has died consented in writing beforehand, or never objected and a listed close relative consents in writing

## The media protocol in practice

- Public relations officers promote awareness and registration, and work with the media.
- Donor families' and recipients' privacy is always protected and paramount.
- Hospitals do not publicise donor details; a family may choose to.
- Time passes before public relations work, to protect grieving families and to avoid celebrating a transplant that may fail.
- Coordinators arrange anonymous thank-you letters. Families are told enough to answer their questions, never enough to identify a recipient.

## ULUNTU, from two sources

**Red File (newest references 2021).** The Uluntu Project: education in under-resourced and vulnerable communities (townships, informal settlements and, when funding allows, rural areas) by culturally similar and culturally sensitive messengers, including in hospitals, clinics and schools. It starts from the fact that the donor pool is not representative of the South African population and that fear and missing information lead to automatic refusals.

**ODF website (read September 2026).** The ULUNTU Awareness Campaign: *uluntu* is isiXhosa for "humanity" and "community". Described as being rolled out in vulnerable communities to break through cultural barriers and increase donor consents. It begins with information-gathering with transplant coordinators from feeder hospitals and community baseline surveys, followed by participative, grassroots outreach.

**Status.** Not confirmed for 2026. No evaluation is available.

## What makes ULUNTU a model worth studying

1. It treats refusal as a problem of information and trust, not unwillingness.
2. It chooses messengers the community will trust.
3. It goes where people already are.
4. It targets consent, and links community work to hospital coordinators.
5. It starts from inequity in the donor pool.

## Questions to ask of any campaign

- Who is it for, and who designed it with them?
- Who delivers the message?
- Does it aim at registration, at consent, or both?
- How would anyone know if it worked?
- Does it respect the answer?
